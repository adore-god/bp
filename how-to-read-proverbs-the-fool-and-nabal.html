How to Read the Proverbs: What the Bible Means by a Fool

Lesson 21 ended on a rule the text gives itself: "as his name is, so is he" (1 Samuel 25:25). The Proverbs run on the same rule with one change. They name almost no people. They give you two types: the one with sense and the fool. This course points you at the Anointed identity (Lesson 24), and the Bible shows you its opposite as well. In the passages below, the opposite is put as foolishness. Nabal's story shows both states in one house, so it is the working example. Then the same pair turns up in Matthew.

The story gives you the two states

You do not have to guess what the wise one and the fool are, because 1 Samuel 25 says it in the BBE:

Now this man was named Nabal, and his wife's name was Abigail: she was a woman of good sense and pleasing looks: but the man was cruel and evil in his ways — 1 Samuel 25:3 (BBE)
Let my lord give no attention to Nabal, that good-for-nothing: for as his name is, so is he, a man without sense — 1 Samuel 25:25 (BBE)
A blessing on your good sense and on you, who have kept me today from the crime of blood — 1 Samuel 25:33 (BBE)

One side has "good sense" and the other is "a man without sense". The one with sense is the one who steps in front of four hundred swords. The one without it has the field, the flock and a full table, and none of it helps him. That is the pair the Proverbs describe.

Put the proverbs next to the scene

From Lesson 21, the scene is a "great man" with "three thousand sheep and a thousand goats" in "Fruitful Field" (25:2), a wife named Source of Joy who has good sense (25:3), and Beloved coming up with swords. Now read the proverbs against it. All quotes are BBE.

A note on 30:22. The BBE says "when his wealth is increased". The Hebrew in the interlinear listings reads "when he is filled with food" (KJV: "filled with meat"). The BBE reads it more broadly here. This lesson does not say the writer of Proverbs 30 had Nabal in mind. The text gives you the same picture twice: a man with a full table and no sense.

Put it on the Genesis 1 map

Lesson 21 showed that Fool has the whole Genesis 1 scene around him: the fruitful ground (day three, Genesis 1:11) and the animals he is given rule over (day six, Genesis 1:26). What is missing is the sense. That is the fool in every proverb above. The scene is complete and the sense is not in it, so the fruit does not supply it (17:16).

The other half of the pair is the one with sense, and in the story that state has a name: Source of Joy. She is not louder or stronger than Beloved with his swords. She is the part of the scene that sees what is about to be done (25:26) and steps in front of it. Then Genesis 1 gives the last part: seed brings forth after its kind (Genesis 1:11). Proverbs 26:27 and 17:13 say that what a state does comes back to it, and in 25:39 the reward of Fool's evil-doing is sent on Fool's own head.

The same pair in Matthew

The Anointed one uses this pair himself. In Matthew 7 he sets out the two states:

Everyone, then, to whom my words come and who does them, will be like a wise man who made his house on a rock And everyone to whom my words come and who does them not, will be like a foolish man who made his house on sand; And the rain came down and there was a rush of waters and the winds were driving against that house; and it came down and great was its fall. — Matthew 7:24, 26-27 (BBE)

In Matthew 25 it comes again, with lamps:

And five of them were foolish, and five were wise. For the foolish, when they took their lights, took no oil with them. But the wise took oil in their vessels with their lights. — Matthew 25:2-4 (BBE)

Set these next to Nabal and the same picture appears three times. The foolish man has a house, the foolish women have lamps, and Fool has a field and a flock. The scene is there. What is missing is the thing that holds it: the rock, the oil, the sense. The two Matthew passages even close alike. In 7:23 the word is "I never had knowledge of you: go from me", and in 25:12 it is "I have no knowledge of you". In both, "Lord, Lord" is said and does not get anyone in (7:21-22, 25:11).

The Greek backs this up. The same two words are used in both Matthew passages: phronimos, "wise" (G5429, Strong's: "thoughtful, sagacious or discreet"), and moros, "foolish" (G3474, Strong's: "dull or stupid, heedless"). Both words are on both lists, at Matthew 7:24, 26 and at 25:2-8. So the wise one and the fool in Matthew are the same pair as in the Proverbs, set against each other.

In Matthew 7 the line between them is plain: the wise man does the words, and the foolish man does not. This matches Nabal. His whole answer is about what he has (25:11), when the question put to him was the name (25:10). In this reading the fool is not the one who lacks anything in the scene. He is the one who does not hold what was said. This lesson claims only what these passages show. The same Greek word for "foolish" is used in other places too, including 1 Corinthians 1 and 3, and those are not covered here.

A short note on the Hebrew

Behind "fool" in the Proverbs there are three Hebrew words. Nabal is Nabal's own word (Strong's H5036, and his name, H5037, is "the same as" it). It is the one used in Proverbs 17:7 and 30:22 above, and in 17:21. The other two are kesil (H3684) and 'evil (H191, say "ev-eel"), and most of the other fool proverbs use them. English has only a word or two for all three, in every translation, so the BBE says "foolish" or "without sense" throughout. Where a proverb uses another word, it shows the same state but not the same word.

How to read a proverb, in four lines:

  1. Find the pair in the story. Who has sense, and who has none?
  2. Put the proverb next to the scene. What does it describe that the story shows?
  3. Put it on the Genesis 1 map. What is in the scene, what is missing, and what comes back after its kind?
  4. Check the word. Look up the Hebrew or Greek before saying two passages share it.

Remember: the Proverbs are names without people. The story of Nabal gives you the two states, the one with sense and the one without. The Anointed one sets out the same pair in Matthew: a wise man on a rock, a foolish man on sand. The fool is a full scene with nothing holding it, and what he does comes back after its kind.
