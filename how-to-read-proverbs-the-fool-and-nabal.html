How to Read the Proverbs: The Fool and Nabal

Lesson 21 ended on a rule the text gives itself: "as his name is, so is he" (1 Samuel 25:25). The Proverbs run on the same rule with one change. They name almost no people. They give you types: the fool, the wise, the one with sense. So the question for a proverb is the question for a name: what state is this word describing? Nabal means "fool", so his story is a working example. You can watch the proverbs happen in it.

Step 1: "Fool" is not one word

In English the Proverbs seem to say "fool" over and over. In Hebrew there are different words behind it. Three matter here (meanings from Strong's, with the Brown-Driver-Briggs lexicon alongside).

Hebrew wordStrong'sStrong's meaningWhere it lives
nabalH5036"stupid; wicked (especially impious)"18 times in the whole Bible. In Proverbs only three: 17:7, 17:21, 30:22
kesilH3684"properly, fat, i.e. (figuratively) stupid or silly"70 times. Most are in Proverbs, a few in Psalms and Ecclesiastes
'evilH191from a root meaning "to be perverse"; "(figuratively) silly"Mostly Proverbs (for example 12:15, 17:28, 20:3, 27:22)

The third word is spelled "evil" in the usual letters, but it is the Hebrew word 'evil (say "ev-eel"), not the English word for wrong. English only has a word or two to cover all three, in every translation: the BBE says "foolish" or "without sense", and the KJV says "fool". Proverbs 17:21 is the clearest case, because the Hebrew puts two different words in one verse: kesil in the first half and nabal in the second. In English both halves read alike:

He who has an unwise son gets sorrow for himself, and the father of a foolish son has no joy. — Proverbs 17:21 (BBE)

Step one for any proverb is to find the Hebrew word behind the English. The state is in the word.

Step 2: Check that the name and the word are one

Nabal's name is listed at H5037. Strong's says it is "the same as" H5036, the word nabal, and the Hebrew is spelled the same (נָבָל). So the name in 1 Samuel 25 and the word in Proverbs 17:7, 17:21 and 30:22 are one word. That is why those three proverbs sit closest to the story. The proverbs that use kesil and 'evil show the same kind of state, but they are different words with their own occurrence lists, and this lesson does not claim more than that.

Step 3: Put the proverb next to the scene

From Lesson 21, the scene is this: a "great man" with "three thousand sheep and a thousand goats" in "Fruitful Field" (25:2), a wife named Source of Joy who has "good sense" (25:3), and Beloved coming up with swords. Now read the proverbs against it. All quotes are BBE.

A note on 30:22. The BBE says "when his wealth is increased". The Hebrew in the interlinear listings reads "when he is filled with food" (KJV: "filled with meat"). The BBE reads it more broadly here. This lesson does not say the writer of Proverbs 30 had Nabal in mind. The text gives you the same picture twice: a man with a full table and no sense.

Step 4: Put it on the Genesis 1 map

Lesson 21 showed that Fool has the whole Genesis 1 scene around him: the fruitful ground (day three, Genesis 1:11) and the animals he is given rule over (day six, Genesis 1:26). What is missing is the sense. That is the fool in every proverb. The scene is complete and the sense is not in it. So Proverbs 17:16 asks how money will get wisdom into the hand of someone who has no sense. The fruit does not supply it.

The other half of the pair is the one with sense. In the story that state has a name: Source of Joy. She is not louder or stronger than Beloved with his swords. She is the part of the scene that sees what is about to be done (25:26) and steps in front of it. In this reading the wise and the fool in Proverbs are two states standing side by side, as in the one house of Nabal. The proverb is a one-line picture of one of them.

Then comes the part Genesis 1 sets up: seed brings forth after its kind (Genesis 1:11). Proverbs 26:27 and 17:13 say the same thing about a state, that what it does comes back to it. In 1 Samuel 25:39 the reward of Fool's evil-doing is sent on Fool's own head. The Judges enforce the state that was held, after its kind.

Two proverbs that seem to disagree

Do not give to the foolish man a foolish answer, or you will be like him. Give a foolish man a foolish answer, or he will seem wise to himself. — Proverbs 26:4-5 (BBE)

Both use kesil, and they sit side by side with no comment. If you read them as rules they cancel out. If you read them as two states they do not. The first warns what happens to you when you answer from the same state: you become like him. In the story, Beloved's answer to Fool's insult is the oath in 25:22, an answer that would have made him the one who judges a whole house for a word. Source of Joy's answer is different: "Let my lord give no attention to Nabal" (25:25), and she says nothing to Fool until dawn (25:36-37). The second warns about the other danger, that the fool is left sure of himself. The proverbs are not instructions to apply one after another. They are pictures of what each state does.

Where this method stops

Nabal's story is Nabal's. It sits closest to the three proverbs that use his word (17:7, 17:21, 30:22). For the many proverbs that use kesil or 'evil, the scene shows the same kind of state but not the same word. When you read one, pull the occurrence list for its own Hebrew word first, then put the proverb next to a scene that uses it.

How to read a proverb, in four lines:

  1. Find the word. Which Hebrew word is behind "fool" or "without sense"?
  2. Check where else it lives. Pull the full occurrence list before saying two passages share a word.
  3. Put the meaning in. The word names the state, as in "as his name is, so is he".
  4. Put it on the Genesis 1 map. What is in the scene, what is missing, and what comes back after its kind?

Remember: the Proverbs are names without people. "Fool" is at least three Hebrew words, and the English keeps them all alike. Nabal is a fool the text names, and the text itself says his name is his nature. His story shows the proverbs acting: a full scene with no sense in it, a word that stops four hundred swords, and what he did coming back on his own head.
