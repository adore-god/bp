Why Did Abraham and Isaac Say "She Is My Sister"? Genesis 2:24 in Three Marriages

People often ask why Abraham told the Egyptians that Sarai was his sister, and why Isaac did the same thing about Rebekah years later. Read on its own, it looks like a man afraid for his life. Read as three marriages told in a row, it looks like something else. Genesis 2:24 gives the rule: a man goes away from his father and his mother and is joined to his wife. Abraham, Isaac and Jacob are each shown taking a wife, and in each story the old family words are still being said. Abraham and Isaac call the wife a sister. Jacob goes to his mother's brother and is told "you are my bone and my flesh". This page reads the three stories in order, with the BBE and Strong's checked, and asks the same three things of each: what is left behind, what comes along, and what word does the story use for the woman?

The passages with the meanings put in:

And the Existing One (Lord) sent great troubles on Pharaoh's house because of Ruling (Sarai), Abram's wife. (Genesis 12:17) So Isaac went on living in Rolling-country (Gerar); ... for, he said, the men of the place may put me to death on account of Fettering-by-beauty (Rebekah); because she is very beautiful. (Genesis 26:6-7) ... Abimelech, king of the Philistines, looking through a window, saw Laughter (Isaac) laughing (playing) with Fettering-by-beauty (Rebekah) his wife. (Genesis 26:8) Now White (Laban) had two daughters: the name of the older was Weary (Leah), and the name of the younger was Ewe (Rachel). (Genesis 29:16)

The meanings used:

  • Sarai (H8297): Strong's gives "dominative", from H8269. The Blue Letter Bible outline gives "princess". The text changes the name to Sarah at Genesis 17:15 (H8283), and the chapter 20 story uses Sarah.
  • Gerar (H1642): Strong's gives "a rolling country". BDB gives "a lodging place". It is used in 10 verses. In Genesis, outside the border line in 10:19, it appears only in the chapter 20 and chapter 26 stories.
  • Isaac (H3327): Strong's gives "laughter (i.e. mockery)", from tsachaq (H6711), "to laugh outright (in merriment or scorn); by implication, to sport". H6711 is used 13 times. Genesis 26:8 is the only use where Isaac is the one doing it. The BBE says "playing".
  • Rebekah (H7259): Strong's gives "fettering (by beauty)", from an unused root "probably meaning to clog by tying up the fetlock". The Blue Letter Bible outline gives "ensnarer". Used 30 times.
  • Rachel (H7354): "ewe", the same as H7353, from an unused root meaning "to journey", a ewe "as a good traveller".
  • Leah (H3812): Strong's gives "weary", from H3811.
  • Laban (H3837): the same as H3836, "white". Used 55 times.
  • Egypt: Lesson 9 gives "double straits". That meaning was not re-checked for this page.
  • Joined in 2:24 (dabaq, H1692): "to cling, cleave". Used 54 times, and Genesis 2:24 is the first.
  • Joined in 29:34 (lavah, H3867): Strong's gives "properly, to twine, i.e. (by implication) to unite, to remain". Used 26 times, and Genesis 29:34 is the only use in Genesis. The name Levi (H3878) is built from it.

The rule being tested

For this cause will a man go away from his father and his mother and be joined to his wife; and they will be one flesh. — Genesis 2:24 (BBE)

This is the leave-and-join rule from Lesson 8. The old, familiar state is left. The wife is the new identity being taken on, and the two are one flesh. The Song of Solomon shows the two words side by side. The call to come away is made to "my bride" (4:8, BBE), and then she is "my sister, my bride" (4:9, 4:12) and again in 5:1. The three stories below are the book's first named marriages, and the word "sister" is still being said in two of them.

So there are two things to watch in each story. One is which house the man is standing in. The other is the word the story uses for the woman.

Abraham and Sarai: going away, and the word "sister"

The going away is told first and plainly:

Now the Lord said to Abram, Go out from your country and from your family and from your father's house, into the land to which I will be your guide: — Genesis 12:1 (BBE)

He goes, and "Lot went with him" (12:4). He takes Sarai and Lot (12:5). Then comes a famine, Egypt, and a sentence to his wife:

And I am certain that when the men of Egypt see you, they will say, This is his wife: and they will put me to death and keep you. Say, then, that you are my sister, and so it will be well with me because of you, and my life will be kept safe on your account. — Genesis 12:12-13 (BBE)

The word "wife" is the one he is afraid of, and "sister" is the one he asks for. She is taken into Pharaoh's house, and the Existing One sends "great troubles on Pharaoh's house because of Sarai, Abram's wife" (12:17). The narrator's word for her is still wife. Then Pharaoh says what the court has been saying:

Why did you say that she was your sister? so that I took her for my wife: now, take your wife and go on your way. — Genesis 12:19 (BBE)

The story runs a second time in Gerar, in chapter 20, with Abimelech. This time the court speaks in a dream, and its sentence is the same word: "for she is a man's wife" (20:3). Abraham's own explanation in 20:12-13 is the most useful part for this page:

And, in fact, she is my sister, the daughter of my father, but not the daughter of my mother; and she became my wife: And when God sent me wandering from my father's house, I said to her, Let this be the sign of your love for me; wherever we go, say of me, He is my brother. — Genesis 20:12-13 (BBE)

Two things stand out. First, he ties the sister word to the time of leaving: it was when he was sent wandering from his father's house that he asked her to say "He is my brother". Second, he places her in the house he was told to leave: she is "the daughter of my father". So the step away from the father's house is real, and the woman who comes with him is described by the father's house. Abimelech's answer is to give back "his wife Sarah" (20:14).

Isaac and Rebekah: the wife who comes out as a sister

Abraham's orders for Isaac's marriage keep the old ground in view. The servant is to get a wife "into my country and to my relations" (24:4), and "do not let my son go back to that land" (24:6). When Rebekah is asked, she answers for herself:

Are you ready to go with this man? And she said, I am ready. So they sent their sister Rebekah and her servant with Abraham's servant and his men. And they gave Rebekah their blessing, saying, O sister, may you be the mother of thousands and ten thousands — Genesis 24:58-60 (BBE)

Her family sends her out as "sister", twice in two verses. Genesis 25:20 repeats it when she becomes Isaac's wife: she is "the sister of Laban the Aramaean". Then the marriage itself:

And Isaac took Rebekah into his tent and she became his wife; and in his love for her, Isaac was comforted after his father's death. — Genesis 24:67 (BBE)

A note on this verse. The BBE says "his tent" and "his father's death". The Hebrew text reads "the tent, Sarah his mother" and "after his mother", and the KJV follows it: Isaac brought her into his mother Sarah's tent and was comforted after his mother's death. So in the Hebrew the wife is brought into the mother's tent. The text does not say here that Isaac went away from his mother. It says the wife came in, and the comfort is for the mother.

The sister word comes back in Gerar, the same place as Abraham's second story:

So Isaac went on living in Gerar; And when he was questioned by the men of the place about his wife, he said, She is my sister; fearing to say, She is my wife; for, he said, the men of the place may put me to death on account of Rebekah; because she is very beautiful. — Genesis 26:6-7 (BBE)

His stated reason is her beauty, and her name is "fettering (by beauty)" (H7259). The story that follows is short and has no dream and no troubles:

And when he had been there for some time, Abimelech, king of the Philistines, looking through a window, saw Isaac playing with Rebekah his wife. And he said to Isaac, It is clear that she is your wife: why then did you say, She is my sister? — Genesis 26:8-9 (BBE)

"Playing" is the verb tsachaq (H6711), the root of his own name, Isaac, "laughter". What shows who she is to him is Isaac doing what his name says, with her. After that, the next lines are about seed: "Isaac, planting seed in that land, got in the same year fruit a hundred times as much" (26:12). The after its kind rule from Lesson 7 says what is planted gives back only what it is.

Jacob, Leah and Rachel: going to the mother's side

Jacob's going away is also told plainly: "So Jacob went out from Beer-sheba to go to Haran" (28:10). But the direction is set by his mother and father, and it points to her family. Rebekah says "go quickly to Haran, to my brother Laban" (27:43). Isaac says:

But go to Paddan-aram, to the house of Bethuel, your mother's father, and there get yourself a wife from the daughters of Laban, your mother's brother. — Genesis 28:2 (BBE)

Haran is the place Abram went away from (12:4), and Jacob is sent back to it. His own oath at Beth-el names the aim: "So that I come again to my father's house in peace" (28:21). When he gets there, Laban, who is Rebekah's brother, says:

Truly, you are my bone and my flesh. ... Because you are my brother are you to be my servant for nothing? — Genesis 29:14-15 (BBE)

"Bone" and "flesh" are the words of Genesis 2:23, where the man says of the woman "This is now bone of my bone and flesh of my flesh". Here the BBE has them said between an uncle and his sister's son, together with the word "brother". The wife Jacob came for is described in family words before he has met her as one.

Then there are two women, an older and a younger:

Now Laban had two daughters: the name of the older was Leah, and the name of the younger was Rachel. And Leah's eyes were clouded, but Rachel was fair in face and form. — Genesis 29:16-17 (BBE)

Jacob loves Rachel and works seven years for her. In the morning after the wedding night he finds it was Leah, and Laban says "In our country we do not let the younger daughter be married before the older" (29:26). The man who was told "he has twice taken my place" (27:36) is now held to the older-first rule himself. He works seven more years, and "his love for her was greater than his love for Leah" (29:30).

Leah's sons' names then carry her side of 2:24. The one verse in Genesis that uses the verb lavah (H3867), "be joined", is hers:

And she was with child again, and gave birth to a son; and said, Now at last my husband will be united to me, because I have given him three sons: so he was named Levi. — Genesis 29:34 (BBE)

This is not the verb of 2:24. That is dabaq (H1692). It is a different word for the same hoped-for joining, and it is spoken by the woman, about a husband who is not yet joined to her. She says it again at Zebulun: "now at last will I have my husband living with me" (30:20). Between the two women the word "sister" turns up as well. Rachel is "full of envy of her sister" (30:1), and later she says "I have had a great fight with my sister, and I have overcome her" (30:8).

The two women are also the ones who say the father's house no longer holds them:

Then Rachel and Leah said to him in answer, What part or heritage is there for us in our father's house? Are we not as people from a strange country to him? — Genesis 31:14-15 (BBE)

In the same chapter the text says that "Rachel secretly took the images of the gods of her father's house" (31:19), and Laban tells Jacob "your heart's desire is for your father's house" (31:30). Rachel sits on the images in the camels' basket and Laban does not find them (31:34). So the leaving is said aloud by both women, and the father's house still goes along with one of them.

What the three stories keep returning to

In this course the wife is the identity a man takes on, and "sister" is the familiar state he has known all along. Genesis 2:24 asks for the leaving first and the joining second. Look at where each story puts the weight:

  • In every one of the three, the going away is told as a journey. It is quick, and it is plain.
  • The joining is where the old words show up. "Sister" for Sarai and Rebekah, "bone and flesh" and "brother" for Jacob, and Leah's hope to be joined.
  • Whenever someone outside the marriage speaks, the word is wife. Pharaoh says it, the court says it to Abimelech, and Abimelech says it again about Isaac. The narrator uses it too: "Abram's wife", "Rebekah his wife".

The court does not argue with the sister word. It says the other word, and in chapter 20 it holds the whole house still until the wife is given back (20:17-18). That fits the courtroom picture in the big idea: the word that is filed is the word that is enforced. The names run alongside it. Isaac is seen laughing with the one he called sister, and Rebekah is "fettering by beauty" in the verse that gives her beauty as the reason. Rachel is a ewe, "a good traveller", and Leah is "weary". The text does not draw these links out for the reader, so this page does not push them any further than showing them side by side.

A short note on the words

What is checked. The BBE wording of Genesis 12, 20, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30 and 31, and of Song of Solomon 3, 4, 5 and 8, was read from the chapter text. The BBE wording of Genesis 2:24 is taken from Lesson 8. The Strong's entries for H8297, H1642, H3327, H6711, H7259, H7354, H3812, H3837, H3867, H1692 and H269 ("sister", achowth) were read with their verse lists. H269 is the word in Genesis 12:13, 12:19, 20:2 and 20:12, and in Song of Solomon 4:9, 4:10, 4:12 and 5:1-2.

Where the BBE differs from the Hebrew. Genesis 24:67 is the one place. The BBE has "his tent" and "his father's death". The Hebrew text has "Sarah his mother" and "after his mother", as the KJV renders it. This page follows the Hebrew in the paragraph above and says so.

Where the lexicons disagree. BDB adds "perhaps wild-cow" for Leah, so "weary" rests on Strong's alone. Gerar is "a rolling country" in Strong's and "a lodging place" in BDB. Rebekah is "fettering (by beauty)" in Strong's and "ensnarer" in the Blue Letter Bible outline. This page uses Strong's and does not lean on any of the three.

Two different "joined" verbs. Genesis 2:24 uses dabaq (H1692) and Genesis 29:34 uses lavah (H3867). The page treats them as two words for one idea and does not claim they are the same word.

Not yet checked. The Hebrew word for "sister" in 24:60 and 26:7. The Hebrew behind "bone" and "flesh" in 29:14 against 2:23. The meanings of Haran, Paddan-aram, Beer-sheba and Abimelech, and Egypt beyond Lesson 9. The Hebrew for "God" in 20:3, where the BBE says "God". Genesis 35, where Rachel's story ends, was not read for this page.

What is not claimed. The text does not say these three stories were written to answer Genesis 2:24. This page shows the rule and the three marriages next to each other and asks what the text does with the woman's name.

Where this sits in the storyline

Genesis 2:23-24 gives the rule. Genesis 12 takes a man out of his father's house, and the three marriages that follow are the first named tests of the rule in one family. Each of them has a sister word, a family word, or a father's house near the joining. The Song of Solomon keeps the same two words together, "my sister, my bride" (4:9, 4:12; 5:1), and has a mother's house at the joining (3:4; 8:2), as Isaac's wife is brought into his mother's tent in the Hebrew of 24:67. Its first two chapters are read on the Song of Solomon page. The same pairing of older and younger women comes back at Judah and Tamar, and in the sisters of Ezekiel 16. Lesson 9 picks up the one piece of ground Abraham owns, Machpelah, where husbands and wives are put to rest in pairs (49:31).

Jacob, Leah and Rachel are read from the well to Rachel's resting-place on the Jacob, Leah and Rachel page.

A question to ask of any story: which house is the man standing in, and what word does the story call the woman?

Remember: Genesis 2:24 says go away and be joined. Abraham and Isaac each call the wife a sister, and the court and the king each answer with the word wife. Jacob goes to his mother's brother and is called bone, flesh and brother. Leah's one word for being joined is spoken by her, about a husband who is not yet joined to her.
