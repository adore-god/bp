What Does the Song of Solomon Mean? Song of Solomon 1-2

People ask whether the Song of Solomon is a love poem, a picture of the church, or a book that does not belong in the Bible. In this course it is read as a scene inside the reader, set against Genesis 1. The Beloved is you aware right now, the companion is the identity you are taking on, and the daughters of Founded-peaceful are the many voices around. The scene is built from the same list Genesis 1 uses: the dark, plants, times, a bird, land creatures. Then it does what Genesis 2:23-24 does: it names, it says leave, and it says joined. Read the passage first, then read it again with the meanings put in.

The passage (BBE, selected verses from Song of Solomon 1-2):

The song of Songs, which is Solomon's. Let him give me the kisses of his mouth: for his love is better than wine. Sweet is the smell of your perfumes; your name is as perfume running out; so the young girls give you their love. Take me to you, and we will go after you: the king has taken me into his house. We will be glad and full of joy in you, we will give more thought to your love than to wine: rightly are they your lovers. I am dark, but fair of form, O daughters of Jerusalem, as the tents of Kedar, as the curtains of Solomon. Let not your eyes be turned on me, because I am dark, because I was looked on by the sun; my mother's children were angry with me; they made me the keeper of the vine-gardens; but my vine-garden I have not kept. Say, O love of my soul, where you give food to your flock, and where you make them take their rest in the heat of the day; why have I to be as one wandering by the flocks of your friends? If you have not knowledge, O most beautiful among women, go on your way in the footsteps of the flock, and give your young goats food by the tents of the keepers. My love is to me as a branch of the cypress-tree in the vine-gardens of En-gedi. See, you are fair, my love, you are fair; you have the eyes of a dove. See, you are fair, my loved one, and a pleasure; our bed is green. Cedar-trees are the pillars of our house; and our boards are made of fir-trees. I am a rose of Sharon, a flower of the valleys. As the lily-flower among the thorns of the waste, so is my love among the daughters. As the apple-tree among the trees of the wood, so is my loved one among the sons. I took my rest under his shade with great delight, and his fruit was sweet to my taste. I say to you, O daughters of Jerusalem, by the roes of the field, do not let love be moved till it is ready. My loved one said to me, Get up, my love, my fair one, and come away. For, see, the winter is past, the rain is over and gone; The flowers are come on the earth; the time of cutting the vines is come, and the voice of the dove is sounding in our land; O my dove, you are in the holes of the mountain sides, in the cracks of the high hills; let me see your face, let your voice come to my ears; for sweet is your voice, and your face is fair. Take for us the foxes, the little foxes, which do damage to the vines; our vines have young grapes. My loved one is mine, and I am his: he takes his food among the flowers. Till the evening comes, and the sky slowly becomes dark, come, my loved one, and be like a roe on the mountains of Bether. — Song of Solomon 1:1-8, 14-17; 2:1-3, 7, 10-12, 14-17 (BBE)

The meanings used: Solomon is Shelomoh (H8010), "peaceful". Jerusalem is Yerushalaim (H3389), "founded peaceful". Kedar is Qedar (H6938), "dusky (of the skin or the tent)", a son of Ishmael (Genesis 25:13). En-gedi is "fountain of a kid" (H5872). Sharon is "plain" (H8289). Bether is the same word as bether (H1335), "a section, part, piece". "Guard" is natar (H5201). "Pasture" and "shepherds" are ra'ah (H7462), "to pasture, tend". "Beloved" is dod (H1730), from a root meaning "to boil, i.e. to love"; David (H1732) comes from the same word and means "loving". "Companion" is ra'yah (H7474), "a female associate". "Meadow-saffron" is the word the KJV gives as "rose" (H2261, "probably meadow-saffron"). "Dove" is yonah (H3123). "Burrowers" is shual (H7776), "a jackal (as a burrower)", the word the KJV gives as "foxes". "Great delight" is chamad (H2530), "to delight in". "Go for yourself" is the Hebrew idiom lekhi lakh.

Six notes on the meanings. First, the BBE uses "love" for two different Hebrew words. It is ra'yah (H7474) in 1:9, 1:15, 2:2, 2:10 and 2:13, always said to her. It is dod (H1730) in 1:14, 1:16, 2:3 and 2:8-17, always said of him. This page reads the Hebrew words, not the English. Second, "rose" is uncertain. Strong's says "probably meadow-saffron", and the word is used only twice, here and in Isaiah 35:1 (H2261). The lily is shoshan (H7799), "a lily (from its whiteness)". Third, some English words match Genesis but the Hebrew words do not, so this page rests on the picture and not the word. The BBE has "thorns" in Song 2:2 (chowach, H2336) and "Thorns and waste plants" in Genesis 3:18 (qots, H6975). It has "dark" in Genesis 1:2 and Song 1:5. It has "keep" in Song 1:6 and "take care of it" in Genesis 2:15. It has "a pleasure" in Song 1:16 and Eden is "pleasure". The page does not claim any of these pairs is one Hebrew word. Fourth, translations differ in three places and the page does not lean on them. In 1:7 the BBE says "wandering", and the NASB95 says "like one who veils herself". In 2:17 the BBE says "Till the evening comes, and the sky slowly becomes dark", and the KJV says "Until the day break, and the shadows flee away". Bether is "a (craggy) place" in Strong's, and "cleft" or "separation" in BDB. Fifth, the BBE has no speaker labels in the verses. The headings in editions are added, so this page tells who speaks from the words used. Also, the BBE "I am" in 1:5 and 2:1 is the ordinary Hebrew "I" (ani, H589), not the verb of Exodus 3:14. This page reads those lines by where they sit in the scene. Sixth, the words "Lord" and "God" do not appear in these two chapters in the BBE. The Judges are not named, and what stands in for them is the Genesis 1 list the scene keeps drawing from.

Who is in the scene

The text names a king, a speaker who is dark, a Beloved, the daughters of Jerusalem, her mother's children and some foxes. In this course's reading, where the many voices are gathered under one present awareness (see the court language), all of them are in you. Male and female here are not about gender. They are in the reader, as in the leave and cleave lesson.

The text names the daughters, the mother's children and the foxes. Reading them as voices and states in you is this course's reading, not something the verses say.

How to read it, step by step:

  1. The name over the song. The song is "Solomon's" (1:1), and Solomon is "peaceful". The daughters belong to Jerusalem, "founded peaceful". So the scene is written in the name of peace, and the voices live in a city named for it. Then: "your name is as perfume running out; so the young girls give you their love" (1:3, BBE). A name is poured out and the voices answer to what it is. That is the rule in 1 Samuel 25: as his name is, so is he. In 1:5 she sets two names side by side: "as the tents of Kedar, as the curtains of Solomon". She is dusky like a tent and fair like the curtains of Peaceful, in one verse.
  2. The dark, and the vine-garden she has not kept. "I am dark... because I was looked on by the sun; my mother's children were angry with me; they made me the keeper of the vine-gardens; but my vine-garden I have not kept" (1:5-6, BBE). Genesis 1 starts in the same place: "it was dark on the face of the deep" (1:2, BBE). The page rests on that picture, not on a shared word (note 3). The job she was given is the man's job in Genesis 2:15: "to do work in it and take care of it" (BBE). She does it, but in gardens that are not hers, and the ones who set her to it are "my mother's children". Genesis 2:24 (BBE) says "a man will go away from his father and his mother". The mother's house is the old state, and it keeps her busy with its own vines.
  3. The shepherd and the two names. She asks "where you give food to your flock" (1:7, BBE), and he answers "go on your way in the footsteps of the flock, and give your young goats food by the tents of the keepers" (1:8, BBE). The verb behind "give food" and "keepers" is ra'ah (H7462), "to pasture, tend". It is the same verb as in "Abel was a keeper of sheep" (Genesis 4:2, BBE), and it comes back in 2:16. So the Beloved is a shepherd, and she is told to bring her young goats to the shepherds' tents. He names her "my love", ra'yah, a female associate. She names him "my loved one", dod, from the root "to boil, to love", the same word David is built from, which the Nabal lesson reads as Beloved. Both are named from the Genesis 1 list: she is a horse (1:9, day six), a dove (1:15, day five) and a lily (2:2, day three), and he is a cypress branch (1:14), an apple-tree (2:3) and a roe (2:9, 2:17). Each name is a kind, and a kind gives back what it is.
  4. The green house. "The vine-gardens of En-gedi" (1:14), "fountain of a kid". Then "our bed is green. Cedar-trees are the pillars of our house; and our boards are made of fir-trees" (1:16-17, BBE). That is day three: "Let grass come up on the earth, and plants producing seed, and fruit-trees giving fruit, in which is their seed, after their sort" (Genesis 1:11, BBE). The bed is grass, and the house is trees, each after its sort. Where she had a vine-garden she did not keep (1:6), here there is a bed and a house of day-three plants. Unlike Matthew 8:20, here there is somewhere to put the head.
  5. The tree and the sitting. "I am a rose of Sharon, a flower of the valleys" (2:1, BBE). Sharon is "plain", so she grows on level ground. Then "the lily-flower among the thorns of the waste" (2:2, BBE), which is the cursed ground of Genesis 3:18 (BBE: "Thorns and waste plants will come up"), the ground Cain works in Lesson 17. The companion is among the daughters as a lily is among thorns. Then he is "the apple-tree among the trees of the wood", and "I took my rest under his shade with great delight, and his fruit was sweet to my taste" (2:3, BBE). The verb behind "great delight" is chamad (H2530), 28 uses, and it is on the tree in Genesis 2:9 (BBE: "delighting the eye and good for food") and Genesis 3:6 (BBE: "a delight to the eyes, and to be desired to make one wise, she took of its fruit"). Same verb, three times: twice on the tree in Genesis, once here. In Genesis 3:6 she judges by what the eyes see and takes. Here she sits down under the tree and the fruit is sweet. Her hiding comes later, at 2:14, and the answer to it is a call.
  6. The call to leave. He comes "dancing on the mountains, stepping quickly on the hills" (2:8, BBE), stands "on the other side of our wall... looking in at the windows" (2:9, BBE) and says "Get up, my love, my fair one, and come away" (2:10, BBE). The Hebrew is qumi lakh ... ulekhi lakh, "go for yourself", the same go-plus-for-you construction as lekh lekha in Genesis 12:1 (BBE: "Go out from your country and from your family and from your father's house"). It is the leave of Genesis 2:24, and the answer to the mother's children in 1:6. The reason he gives is a list of Genesis 1 parts: the winter is past (times, "for signs, and for marking the changes of the year", Genesis 1:14, BBE), the flowers are on the earth (day three), and "the voice of the dove" is in the land (day five, Genesis 1:20). Back at 2:7 the daughters were told "do not let love be moved till it is ready". This course reads the two together: leave the old state now, and do not move the new one before its time.
  7. The dove in the holes, the burrowers in the vines. "O my dove, you are in the holes of the mountain sides... let me see your face, let your voice come to my ears" (2:14, BBE). Compare Genesis 3:8-9 (BBE): the man and his wife go "to a secret place among the trees of the garden, away from the eyes of the Lord God", and the voice comes, "Where are you?". It is the same two moves, a hiding place and a call to come out. Then "Take for us the foxes, the little foxes, which do damage to the vines; our vines have young grapes" (2:15, BBE). The fox is a burrower, and in Matthew 8:20 its place is a hole. Here the dove, a bird, is in the holes, and the burrowers are in the vines. Each is in the wrong place for its kind, and the call is to bring the dove out and take the foxes. "Take" is a rule over a day-six creature, the job of Genesis 1:26 (BBE: "let him have rule over... the cattle and over all the earth").
  8. Mine and his. "My loved one is mine, and I am his: he takes his food among the flowers" (2:16, BBE). The belonging is said both ways, which is the joining of Genesis 2:24. The shepherd verb is back, and he feeds among plants, as in Genesis 1:30 (BBE): "to every beast of the earth... I have given every green plant for food". The two jobs that split in Genesis 4, the keeper of sheep and the keeper of the ground, meet here. Then the scene ends: "Till the evening comes... come, my loved one, and be like a roe on the mountains of Bether" (2:17, BBE). Bether is the same word as the pieces of the cut animals in Genesis 15:10 (H1335, four uses: Genesis 15:10, Jeremiah 34:18 and 34:19, and here). The page does not lean on which sense is meant. It only notes that the last place named is named for something divided.

What changed from Genesis 1

Lesson 5 says every story is the Genesis 1 picture, changed. Matthew 8 ran it in order. The Song does not. Every part is here, but they come tangled, as in a garden, and the pair come at the end.

So the Song is the original scene nearly rebuilt. It has a ground, a house, a shade and a pair who say "mine" and "his". What is not settled is the time. The fruit is young, the burrowers are in the vines, the dove is still hiding, and love is "not to be moved till it is ready".

The outcome is name, leave, join

Genesis 2:23-24 (BBE) has three moves. The man names her: "let her name be Woman". Then "a man will go away from his father and his mother". Then "be joined to his wife; and they will be one flesh". The Song has the same three, in the same order.

  1. Name. "my love" (1:9, 1:15, 2:2) and "my loved one" (2:3, 2:16). The names are said before anything else is done.
  2. Leave. The mother's children set her to someone else's vines (1:6), and then comes "Get up... and come away" (2:10).
  3. Join. "My loved one is mine, and I am his" (2:16).

In this course the Song shows that step from the inside: name the identity, leave the old state, and say it is yours. It also shows why the fruit is not here yet. The joining is said, and the time is still being held.

Remember: read the names first (Peaceful, Founded-peaceful, Dusky, Beloved, companion), then match each part to Genesis 1 (the dark, the plants, the times, the dove, the creatures), then ask what the scene does with them. Here it names, leaves and joins, and then waits for the time.
