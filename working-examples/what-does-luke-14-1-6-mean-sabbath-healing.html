What Does Luke 14:1-6 Mean? The Sabbath, the Water and the Man Made Well

Luke 14:1-6 is one table, one day and one man. The day is the Sabbath, the house belongs to one of the chief Pharisees (the Greek word is archon, "ruler"), and everyone in it is watching. A man with a disease is there, and Jesus asks the scribes and Pharisees whether it is right to make people well on that day. The Greek word for the first group is "lawyers". The text gives no answer in words. It gives the men keeping still, the man made well and sent away, and a second question about an ox or ass in a water-hole, which no one answers. In this course the scene is read as taking place inside the reader, in the court of Lesson 1. Jesus is the identity you hold, the host is a ruler, the Pharisees and the lawyers are the voices that hold the statute, and Elohim, the court that sits over all of them, are the judges and rulers. The Sabbath is the day the judges' work is finished and the case is at rest (Genesis 2:2). The question in verse 3 is whether the identity can act once the judges are at rest. The scene answers it with what the court does: the voices keep still, the man is cured and freed, and no voice has anything to say against it. This page reads why. Read the passage first, then read it again with the meanings put in.

The passage (BBE):

And it came about that when he went into the house of one of the chief Pharisees on the Sabbath, to have a meal, they were watching him. (Luke 14:1) And a certain man was there who had a disease. (Luke 14:2) And Jesus, answering, said to the scribes and Pharisees, Is it right to make people well on the Sabbath or not? (Luke 14:3) But they said nothing. And he made him well and sent him away. (Luke 14:4) And he said to them, Which of you, whose ox or ass has got into a water-hole, will not straight away get him out on the Sabbath? (Luke 14:5) And they had no answer to that question. (Luke 14:6) — Luke 14:1-6 (BBE)

The same verses with the meanings put in:

And it came about that when he went into the house of one of the rulers of the Separatists on the Intermission, to have a meal, they were watching alongside him. (Luke 14:1) And a certain man was there who had a watery sickness. (Luke 14:2) And Existing-One-is-salvation, answering, said to the law-experts and Separatists, Is it right to make people well on the Intermission or not? (Luke 14:3) But they kept still. And he cured him and freed him fully. (Luke 14:4) And he said to them, Which of you, whose ox or ass has got into a hole dug to hold water, will not straight away draw him up on the Intermission? (Luke 14:5) And they had no answer to that question. (Luke 14:6)

The meanings used:

  • Jesus (G2424): the Greek form of the Hebrew Yehoshua (H3091), which Strong's gives as from H3068 (YHVH, the Existing One) and H3467 (yasha, "to save"). Blue Letter Bible glosses it "Jehovah is salvation". In this course: Existing One is salvation.
  • Pharisee (G5330): "of Hebrew origin (compare parash); a separatist, i.e. exclusively religious". Parash (H6567) is "to separate, literally (to disperse) or figuratively (to specify)". It is used five times in the KJV: Leviticus 24:12, Numbers 15:34, Nehemiah 8:8, Proverbs 23:32 and Ezekiel 34:12.
  • Lawyer (nomikos, G3544): "an expert in the (Mosaic) law". It is used nine times, and Luke 14:3 is one of them. The BBE gives "scribes" there; the KJV gives "lawyers".
  • Sabbath (sabbaton, G4521): "of Hebrew origin (H7676)". H7676 is "intensive from H7673; intermission, i.e. (specifically) the Sabbath". H7673 (shabath) is "to repose, i.e. desist from exertion". Shabath is the word behind "rested" in Genesis 2:2 and 2:3 (KJV).
  • Chief (archon, G758): "a first (in rank or power)", rendered in Strong's as "chief (ruler), magistrate, prince, ruler". The KJV translates it "ruler" 22 times out of 37. Luke 14:1 is on its occurrence list, and Thayer's cites it there for "ruler of the Pharisees". The BBE says "chief", and the Greek word is the one for ruler. Elohim is the plural word for the judges and rulers (Lesson 3).
  • Watch (paratereo, G3906): "to inspect alongside, i.e. note insidiously or scrupulously". It is used six times: Mark 3:2, Luke 6:7, Luke 14:1, Luke 20:20, Acts 9:24 and Galatians 4:10.
  • Disease (hydropikos, G5203): "to be 'dropsical'", from a compound of hudor (water, G5204) and a derivative of optanomai (G3700), "as if looking watery". It is used once, in Luke 14:2. The BBE says "a disease" and does not show the water.
  • Said nothing (hesuchazo, G2270): "to keep still (intransitively), i.e. refrain from labor, meddlesomeness or speech". It is used five times: Luke 14:4, Luke 23:56, Acts 11:18, Acts 21:14 and 1 Thessalonians 4:11. In Luke 23:56 it is the word for resting on the Sabbath (KJV "rested the sabbath day").
  • Took hold (epilambanomai, G1949): "to seize (for help, injury, attainment, or any other purpose)". Luke 14:4 in the Greek has "having taken hold of him", which the BBE does not show. The same word is in Luke 23:26, where Simon is laid hold of (see the previous page).
  • Made well (iaomai, G2390): "to cure (literally or figuratively)". Sent away (apolyo, G630): "to free fully, i.e. (literally) relieve, release, dismiss". Luke 14:4 is on both occurrence lists.
  • Water-hole (phrear, G5421): "a hole in the ground (dug for obtaining or holding water or other purposes)". It is used seven times. Luke 14:5 is one; the KJV gives "pit".
  • Get out (anaspao, G385): "to take up or extricate". It is used only twice: Luke 14:5 and Acts 11:10.

Who is in the scene

The text names a host, watchers, two groups of men who read the statute, a man with water in him, an ox and an ass, and a water-hole. No place is named. The places are the house and the water-hole. In this course's reading, where the many voices are gathered under one present awareness (see the court language), all of them are in you.

This is how the Bible is set up, not an extra layer on top of it. Genesis 1:26 makes man the one made in the judges' own image (Lesson 6), so every scene has the same three parts: you, the identity you claim, and the judges that make it real. Here the identity sits at a table the judges hold, on the one day the judges are at rest.

How to read it, step by step:

  1. The Sabbath is the court at rest, and the statutes are already on file. The day is named first: "on the Sabbath" (14:1). Sabbath is "intermission", from shabath, "to desist from exertion" (H7673). Genesis 1 is where the work is done. The court parts the waters from the waters (1:6, BBE), gathers the waters in one place so the dry land is seen (1:9, BBE), and fixes every creature "after its sort" (1:24-25, BBE; see Rule One). Then Genesis 2:2 says "on the seventh day God came to the end of all his work" (BBE), and 2:3 says "God gave his blessing to the seventh day and made it holy" (BBE), and it is the judges who set it apart (see what holy means). The KJV gives "rested" for shabath in both verses. So the rest means no new statute is made. It does not mean the statutes stop. Every statute on file keeps running. The house is held by a ruler. The Greek word is archon (G758), "a first (in rank or power)", and Strong's gives "ruler" and "magistrate" among its renderings. Elohim is the plural word for the judges and rulers, so the host is one of the court's own, and the table is the court's table. The watchers "were watching him" (paratereo, G3906, "note insidiously or scrupulously"). The Pharisees are "separatists", and separating is what the court itself did on day two. In Render Unto Caesar the separatists put a question to him, and here he puts the question to them. What they are inspecting is whether the identity at the table adds something new to the finished work.
  2. The man is water, and the statute for water is already written. "A certain man was there who had a disease" (14:2). The Greek word, hydropikos, is used nowhere else in the New Testament. It is "dropsical", from hudor, "water", and "as if looking watery". The sickness is named for the water. Genesis 1:2 opens with "the face of the waters" (BBE), and on day three "Let the waters under the heaven come together in one place, and let the dry land be seen" (1:9, BBE). That is the statute for exactly this condition, and it was written and finished before the Sabbath began. In this reading the man is a state in which the waters are not gathered to their place. He does not need a new statute. He needs the old one applied. That is why the text names the water.
  3. The question is answered by what the court does. "Is it right to make people well on the Sabbath or not?" (14:3). The question is put as a rule: "Is it right...?" But the day begins as a state the judges declare, not a rule given to the man (what holy means), so what the question really asks is whether applying a finished statute counts as new work. The answer comes in three acts. First, "they said nothing" (14:4). The Greek word, hesuchazo (G2270), means "to keep still... refrain from labor, meddlesomeness or speech", and in Luke 23:56 it is the word for resting on the Sabbath, in the same gospel, after the walk to Golgotha. The voices that hold the statute keep the rest, and their stillness is their agreement. Second, he "made him well". The Greek also says he took hold of the man first (epilambanomai, G1949, "to seize"), then cured him (iaomai, G2390). The order is the mechanism: the identity takes hold of the state as its own, and then the statute for that state is applied. Third, he "sent him away". The Greek is apolyo (G630), "to free fully, i.e. (literally) relieve, release, dismiss". In court language the case is dismissed. The man is not left in the water and not left on the table. He is released. The same shape is in Daniel 8, where the one who sees is touched and stood on his feet where he had been (8:18). The Hebrew and the Greek words are different, so this is a matching picture, not a word link.
  4. The water-hole proves the statute is the court's own. Jesus puts it to them: "Which of you, whose ox or ass has got into a water-hole, will not straight away get him out on the Sabbath?" (14:5). The word for water-hole, phrear (G5421), is "a hole in the ground (dug for obtaining or holding water or other purposes)". The ox and the ass are creatures fixed "after their sort" (Genesis 1:24-25, BBE), so what has fallen in is something of its own kind that has landed in the waters. The lawyers hold the statute of kinds, and by their own practice a creature of its kind that has fallen into the waters is drawn up "straight away" (anaspao, G385, "to take up or extricate"), on the Sabbath, without waiting for a new decision. The same statute covers the man, because Genesis 1:26 makes man the kind made in the judges' own image. "And they had no answer to that question" (14:6). They have no answer because the answer is their own practice. The statute they hold is the statute that cured the man, so there is nothing for them to rule against, and the identity's filing stands uncontested.

The Genesis words in the scene: what changed

Lesson 5 says every story is the Genesis 1 picture, changed, and the seven days fix the words the whole Bible uses (Lesson 4). Here every object in these six verses is already in Genesis 1 or 2. The scene uses their words and turns each one.

In Genesis the court finishes its work and rests. In Luke 14:1-6 the same parts are all there, but the order is turned: the rest is the day on which a man with water in him is cured, the separators keep still, and the statute applied is the one written on day three.

A short note on the words

Scribes and lawyers. The BBE says "scribes and Pharisees" in 14:3. The Greek word for the first group is nomikos (G3544), "an expert in the (Mosaic) law", and the KJV gives "lawyers". This page uses the Greek meaning and names the BBE word where it quotes.

Disease and dropsy. The BBE says "a disease". The Greek word is used once in the New Testament and means a sickness of water. That is why the page reads the man against the waters of Genesis 1.

Parting and separatists. The BBE says "parting" in Genesis 1:6. The Hebrew word there was not checked against parash (H6567), so the page links the two by what the court and the Pharisees do, not by a shared word.

Where this sits in the storyline

This is Genesis 1 and 2 run forward. Six days of statutes are fixed, and the seventh is the court at rest. At a table held by a ruler, the voices that separate and hold the statute watch, and when the question comes they keep still. In the stillness the identity takes hold of a state with water in it, the day-three statute is applied, and the state is released. The water-hole question shows why no one can object: the statute that cured the man is the one they would use themselves. In this reading the Sabbath is not a pause in enforcement. It is the condition in which only what is already on file is enforced, and that is why the cure is uncontested.

Remember: the Sabbath is the court at rest, and the statutes it fixed keep running. The man with water in him is a day-three problem, and the day-three statute is already written. The identity takes hold of the state, the voices that hold the statute keep still, the state is cured and released, and no one can answer, because the statute is theirs.
