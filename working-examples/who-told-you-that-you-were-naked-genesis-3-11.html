Who Told You That You Were Naked? Genesis 2:25 to 3:11

Genesis 2:25 says the man and his wife "were without clothing, and they had no sense of shame" (BBE). Between that verse and the court's first question there is a snake, called "wiser than any beast of the field" (3:1). Then the court asks something odd. It does not start with the fruit. It asks, "Who gave you the knowledge that you were without clothing?" (3:11). In the BBE, "without clothing" and "wiser" look like two unrelated ideas. In the Hebrew they are not. The word behind "without clothing" in 2:25 and the word behind "wiser" in 3:1 are built on the same four consonants, and Strong's puts both, and the word for "naked" in 3:7-11, under one root that means bare and, from there, shrewd. This page reads the passage first as written, then again with the meanings put in, and asks why the court's question is about the knowledge and not the clothes.

The passage with the meanings put in:

And the man and his wife were bare (without clothing), and they had no sense of shame. (Genesis 2:25) Now the snake was more shrewd (wiser) than any beast of the field which the Existing One of the Judges (Lord God) had made. (Genesis 3:1) And their eyes were open and they were conscious that they were bare (they had no clothing) and they made themselves coverings (coats of leaves stitched together). (Genesis 3:7) And he said, Hearing your voice in the garden I was full of fear, because I was bare (without clothing): and I kept myself from your eyes. (Genesis 3:10) And he said, Who gave you the knowledge that you were bare (without clothing)? Have you taken of the fruit of the tree which I said you were not to take? (Genesis 3:11)

The meanings used:

  • Naked in 2:25 (arom, H6174): Strong's gives "nude, either partially or totally", from aram (H6191) "in its original sense". It is used 16 times, and Genesis 2:25 is the first. The form in 2:25 is the plural, arummim. The BBE says "without clothing".
  • Naked in 3:7, 3:10 and 3:11 (eyrom, H5903): Strong's gives "nudity", also from aram (H6191). It is used 10 times: Genesis 3:7, 3:10, 3:11, Deuteronomy 28:48 and six verses in Ezekiel. This is a different Hebrew word from the one in 2:25, although the BBE says "without clothing" or "no clothing" in all four verses.
  • Wiser (arum, H6175): Strong's gives "passive participle of H6191; cunning (usually in a bad sense)" and the KJV renders it "crafty, prudent, subtil". It is used 11 times in 11 verses: Genesis 3:1, Job 5:12 and 15:5, and Proverbs 12:16, 12:23, 13:16, 14:8, 14:15, 14:18, 22:3 and 27:12. The KJV has "prudent" 8 times, "crafty" twice and "subtil" once. So Strong's "usually in a bad sense" does not match the count: 8 of the 11 uses are the good side.
  • The root (aram, H6191): Strong's gives "properly, to be (or make) bare; but used only in the derived sense (through the idea perhaps of smoothness) to be cunning (usually in a bad sense)".
  • Eden (H5731): "pleasure", the named ground from The Original Plot of Genesis 1.
  • Sin (chatta'ah, H2403): used 294 times. The first is Genesis 4:7.

The scene before anything is said

Genesis 2:24 is the leave-and-join rule from Lesson 8: a man goes away from the old state and is joined to his wife, and they are one flesh. Verse 25 comes straight after it, and it gives two facts. Nothing is covering them, and there is no shame. They are bare, and the bareness costs nothing, because there is no gap between what they are and what they see. That is the original scene, set on the ground named Eden, "pleasure".

One more fact from the same chapter matters later. The man has already named the animals:

And the man gave names to all cattle and to the birds of the air and to every beast of the field — Genesis 2:20 (BBE)

Keep "every beast of the field" in mind. Genesis 3:1 uses the same group to place the snake.

The same word family, one verse apart

VerseThe BBE saysThe HebrewWhat it means
2:25"without clothing"arummim (H6174)bare
3:1"wiser"arum (H6175)shrewd, cunning, prudent
3:7, 3:10, 3:11"no clothing", "without clothing"eyrummim, eyrom (H5903)bare, nudity

All three trace to one root in Strong's, aram (H6191): to be bare, and from that, to be cunning. The man and his wife are arummim in 2:25. The snake is arum in 3:1. The BBE hides this by saying "wiser", and the KJV hides it too by saying "subtil". A reader of either never sees that the text has set the two words side by side.

So one word family covers two states. Bare is the state with nothing held back and nothing worked out. Shrewd is the state that has worked out how things look and what to do about it. In this course the scene is read as taking place inside the reader, and the snake is not an outsider. It is one of the "beasts of the field" the man had named (2:20), made in the same scene (3:1). The snake lesson covers that part. This page follows only the two words.

What the shrewd voice leads to

Watch what the text shows happening after the shrewd voice speaks. It is the same move each time.

VerseWhat the text shows
3:6The woman sees that the tree is "good for food, and a delight to the eyes, and to be desired to make one wise". The test is how it looks
3:7Their eyes are open, they are "conscious that they had no clothing", and they make "coats of leaves stitched together"
3:8They go "to a secret place among the trees of the garden, away from the eyes of the Lord God"
3:10"I kept myself from your eyes"

3:21 is not part of this move. It comes after the court's questions and verdicts (3:9-19) and the name Eve (3:20), and it is the court, not the pair, that makes the covering: "The Lord God made for Adam and for his wife coats of skins for their clothing" (BBE). The fig leaves and skins page goes through the two coverings word by word.

The move is cover, then hide. Nothing in the chapter is done to the bareness itself. What is new is that the bareness is now something to be looked at, and so it is covered and kept from sight. The shrewd word does the same job in Proverbs. In 12:16 and 12:23 the one called arum is the one who conceals: the NASB listing tags the verb H3680 and renders it "conceals". This page does not say it is the same verb as in Genesis 3. It is a matching picture, set beside the Genesis 3 coverings.

The word is two-sided. Genesis 3:1 and Job 5:12 and 15:5 use it for the crafty. Proverbs uses it eight times, and the KJV renders every one of them "prudent". The shrewd state is not itself a villain. It is the state that works out how things look.

The question the court asks

When the court comes into the scene it asks "Where are you?" (3:9). Then, in 3:11, it asks two things:

Who gave you the knowledge that you were without clothing? Have you taken of the fruit of the tree which I said you were not to take? — Genesis 3:11 (BBE)

The second question is about the fruit. The first is about the claim. Being bare was never called a problem in 2:25. What is new is the knowledge that being bare is something to hide, and the court asks where that knowledge came from. In this course's terms, a claim has been filed and the court asks who filed it.

The answers go sideways. The man speaks to the fruit: "The woman whom you gave to be with me, she gave me the fruit of the tree and I took it" (3:12). The woman points to the creature: "I was tricked by the deceit of the snake and I took it" (3:13, both BBE). That sends the reader back to 3:1. The snake whose word is arum is the one named when the court looks for where the knowledge came from.

Where the word "sin" is, and is not

The BBE of Genesis 3 never uses the word "sin", and it calls the creature a "snake" all the way through. The word first turns up in the next chapter, at Cain's door:

If you do well, will you not have honour? and if you do wrong, sin is waiting at the door, desiring to have you, but do not let it be your master. — Genesis 4:7 (BBE)

The noun is H2403 (chatta'ah), used 294 times, and the first of them is Genesis 4:7. So the order the text gives is: bare, shrewd, covered, hidden, questioned, and only later the word sin. The Cain lesson reads the door and the offering. This page checked the noun H2403 only, not the verb or the adjective for sin.

A short note on the words

What is checked. The BBE wording of Genesis 2:20, 2:25, 3:1-24 and 4:7 was read from the verse text. The word lists for H6174, H6175, H5903 and H2403 were read as occurrence lists. The lists for H6174 and H6175 show forms spelled with the same consonants (ערומים and ערום), and only the later vowel and dot marks differ.

The two naked words. The BBE gives "without clothing" for 2:25, 3:10 and 3:11, and "no clothing" for 3:7. In the Hebrew, 2:25 uses H6174 and 3:7-11 use H5903. They are two words from one family, not one word repeated.

The root is not settled. Strong's derives all three words from aram (H6191). The NAS Exhaustive Concordance lists H6174 and H5903 as "from ur" instead. The lexicons disagree, so this page does not rest on the root. It rests on what the text itself shows: arummim in 2:25, arum in 3:1, and the covering and hiding that follow.

What is not claimed. The text does not say the two words were chosen as a play on sound, and this page does not say so. It shows that both are in the text, one verse apart. The Proverbs 12:16 and 12:23 picture is by reference only, because the BBE wording of those two verses was not pulled for this page.

Where this sits in the storyline

Genesis 1 builds an open scene. Genesis 2 ends with the man and his wife bare in it, with no shame. Genesis 3 puts a shrewd creature from the same field beside them, and the next thing the text shows is covering and hiding. The court's first question looks for where the knowledge came from. After the verdicts (3:14-19) and the name Eve (3:20), the Lord God makes a covering of his own (3:21), which is a different act from the pair's leaves. In Genesis 4 the first word for sin appears at Cain's door. The temple curtain page picks up this same chapter from the other end, at the winged ones put at the east of Eden (3:24).

A question to ask of any story: who is bare, who is shrewd, and what gets covered?

Remember: in Genesis 2:25 the man and his wife are bare (arummim, H6174), and in 3:1 the snake is shrewd (arum, H6175). Strong's puts both, and the naked words of 3:7-11 (H5903), under one root: bare, and from that, cunning. After the shrewd voice speaks, the text shows covering and hiding. The court does not ask about the clothes. It asks who gave the knowledge, and the word sin does not appear until Cain's door in 4:7.
