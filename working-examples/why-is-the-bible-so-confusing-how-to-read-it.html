Why Is the Bible So Confusing? Three Reasons and a Way Through

Plenty of thoughtful adults open the Bible, get a few chapters in, and find it stops making sense. A hardened heart that is blamed on the man and then on the Lord. A talking donkey. Two accounts that seem to say different things. Online, the same verses get a dozen different answers, each delivered with confidence. If that sounds familiar, it is not because you are missing some special knowledge. There are three ordinary reasons the Bible comes across as confusing, and each one has a plain fix. This page goes through them, then tests the fix on one well-known puzzle: Pharaoh's heart.

First, the background this page uses

This course reads the Bible against its own opening plot. The short version, with a lesson for each part:

  • The court. The Bible works like a courtroom. You make a claim about who you are, and the judges rule to match it. Lesson 1 sets this out.
  • The opening scene. Genesis 1 builds a whole scene (light, land, plants, lights, creatures) before the man appears, and then the identity is placed in it. Every later story is that scene, changed. Lesson 5 covers it.
  • Rule One: after its kind. What is planted gives back only what it is. Lesson 7.
  • Rule Two: leave and join. A new state is entered by leaving the old one. Lesson 8.
  • Names are codes. A name tells you what state is being lived in, and the story runs out in line with it. Lesson 9.
  • The reader. The people, places and events show how identity works inside the reader. About this reading explains what that means and how you can check it.

Reason 1: it gets read as history, and then it stops making sense

Most of us were taught to treat the Bible as a record of things that happened to other people, long ago and far away. From that seat you are a spectator. A spectator has to explain every odd detail from the outside, and the oddest details have no good outside explanation. So they get filed under "miracle" or "mystery", or they get argued over.

There is a second, quieter problem. Many readers switch between "this is a report of events" and "this is a picture of something deeper" without noticing, sometimes inside a single verse. Each switch changes what counts as an answer, so the reading never settles. Nobody is doing this on purpose. It simply happens when no rule has been chosen.

The course keeps one point of view all the way through: the scene is yours. The ground, the people and the events show what is happening to the identity you are holding. Here is what that does to a famous puzzle. The Bible in Basic English (BBE) says of Pharaoh:

But when Pharaoh saw that there was peace for a time, he made his heart hard and did not give ear to them, as the Lord had said. — Exodus 8:15 (BBE)
And the Lord made Pharaoh's heart hard, and he would not give ear to them, as the Lord had said. — Exodus 9:12 (BBE)

Read from the spectator's seat, these two verses compete. Who hardened it, Pharaoh or the Lord? Someone has to be to blame, and the argument goes round and round. Read from the reader's side, there is no competition. The man holds a claim, and the court makes real the kind of thing he holds. That is one event, described once from the claim and once from the court. Both verses even end on the same note: it came out "as the Lord had said" (8:15, 9:12, and again in 9:35). Lesson 18 works this one through name by name.

Reason 2: nothing is checked against the Bible's own plot

If every verse is read on its own, there is nothing to measure a reading against. One person says a verse means this, another says that, and both sound reasonable because neither has a yardstick. The gaps then get filled with speculation, and the louder the speculation the more it spreads.

The Bible does supply its own yardstick, and it is right at the start. Genesis 1 sets out the scene and the rules, and the later books keep using the same words and groups: ground, seed, plants, creatures, lights, the court. A reading can be tested against them. This is the test the course uses for any story:

  • What is the ground, and what does its name mean?
  • Who is standing on it, and what does their name mean?
  • What has changed from the original picture in Genesis 1?

The rule behind the test comes straight from Genesis 1:11, which is why it is Rule One of the course:

And God said, Let grass come up on the earth, and plants producing seed, and fruit-trees giving fruit, in which is their seed, after their sort: and it was so. — Genesis 1:11 (BBE)

A reading that matches the scene, the names and "after its kind" holds together. A reading that needs a new idea brought in from outside to make it work has a loose end. That is what gets tied off by the plot, and not by whoever argues best.

Reason 3: different ways of reading get blended together

This is the one most people never see. A passage can be read as a record of events, as advice for living, as a prediction, as a tradition handed down, or as a scene in the reader. Each of these has its own rules for what counts as a good answer. That is fine on its own. The trouble starts when pieces of several are mixed in one sitting, so that a verse is being read three ways at once and the result belongs to none of them. Add the many versions of each way that exist online, and it is no surprise that people talk past each other.

The fix is a discipline anyone can use:

  1. Choose one set of rules and say what they are.
  2. Apply them from Genesis to Revelation, in every book, without changing them halfway.
  3. Let the text be the check. Read the verse in a plain translation with the chapter around it, put the meanings of the names in, and see whether the reading fits. If it does not fit, the reading changes, not the text.

This course's rules are the ones listed at the top of the page: the court, the opening scene, after its kind, leave and join, and names as codes. You do not have to accept them on trust. Every step can be checked against the verses.

How we know these are the Bible's own rules

A fair question is: why these rules and not others? The answer is that they were not brought to the Bible from outside. They are read straight off its opening chapters. Genesis 1 sets the court and the scene, "after its kind" is spoken in 1:11, leave and join is given in 2:24, and Genesis 3 and 4 show what happens when the claim goes wrong. Nothing is added to those chapters. The rules are simply what they say.

The proof is what happens next. A set of rules is only worth trusting if the book keeps obeying them in places no one planned for. The Bible does. The same court language runs from Genesis to the end: judges and rulers, judgments, law, statutes, commandments, witnesses, testimony. Even the names of the two halves, Old and New Testament, are courtroom words. And the same three questions about ground, name and change work whichever book you open, in the law, the history, the poetry, the prophets and the gospels alike.

This is the difference between a theory and a method. If the rules were invented, they would break somewhere, and a reading would need an extra idea to rescue it. Here the opposite happens: the book keeps behaving the way Genesis said it would, in every book from the first page to the last. When the rules hold in book after book, and the text confirms the reading each time, they are not our rules. They are the Bible's.

Genesis keeps telling you to read the names

There is one more thing the opening chapters do, and it is easy to read past. They keep stopping to name things. The very first acts of the court are namings:

Naming the light, Day, and the dark, Night. — Genesis 1:5 (BBE) And God gave the arch the name of Heaven. — Genesis 1:8 (BBE) And God gave the dry land the name of Earth; and the waters together in their place were named Seas. — Genesis 1:10 (BBE)

Then the naming is handed to the man, and the text takes care to say what a name is:

And from the earth the Lord God made every beast of the field and every bird of the air, and took them to the man to see what names he would give them: and whatever name he gave to any living thing, that was its name. — Genesis 2:19 (BBE)

"That was its name" is the point. In Genesis a name is not a label stuck on afterwards. It says what the thing is. And from here on, whenever a name is given, a reason comes with it:

  • The woman: "let her name be Woman because she was taken out of Man" (2:23).
  • Eve: "because she was the mother of all who have life" (3:20).
  • Seth: "God has given me another seed in place of Abel" (4:25).
  • Noah: "he will give us rest from our trouble and the hard work of our hands" (5:29).

The first book of the Bible is teaching you how to read, and what it teaches is that the meaning sits in the name. That is why this course puts the meanings of names and places into every passage. It is following a habit the text starts in its first chapter and never drops. The story is carried in the meanings, so a reader who skips them is reading only the outside of it.

What this means for you as the reader

If the Bible has felt like a book that argues with itself, that is a sign of mixed rules, not of a defect in you. Once the rules are fixed and the point of view is yours, contradictions that looked permanent tend to turn out to be one event seen from two sides, as with Pharaoh's heart. You also stop needing someone else's confident answer, because you have a way to check any reading yourself.

Three questions to ask of any passage: Whose scene is this? What is the ground, and what has changed from Genesis 1? Am I using one set of rules, or switching?

Remember: the Bible is confusing mostly when it is read as other people's history, with no plot to check against, and with several ways of reading mixed together. Keep one point of view, test against Genesis 1, and let the text confirm the reading.
