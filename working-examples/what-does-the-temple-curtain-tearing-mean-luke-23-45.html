Why Does the Temple Curtain Tear? Luke 23:45

At the cross the curtain of the Temple is "parted in two" (Luke 23:45, BBE). Matthew and Mark tell it too, and they add "from end to end". None of the three says why. What Luke does give is the order around it: a garden is promised first (23:43), the land goes dark and the light of the sun goes out (23:44-45), the curtain parts, and then the spirit is given up (23:46). Every one of those pieces is already in Genesis. The curtain is the screen between the holy place and the most holy, and Exodus 26:33 uses the same Hebrew word for it that Genesis 1:6 uses for the arch that parts the waters from the waters. It also carries the winged ones that Genesis 3:24 puts at the east of Eden to keep the way to the tree of life. In this course the scene is read as taking place inside the reader. The curtain is the edge between where you are now and where you are heading, and the parting is that edge being opened. John is the odd one out: he has no curtain at all, and the reason says a lot (see "Why John has no curtain" below). Read the passage first, then read it again with the meanings put in.

The passage with the meanings put in:

And he said to him, Truly I say to you, Today you will be with me in the Eden-garden (Paradise). (Luke 23:43) And it was now about the sixth hour; and all the land was dark till the ninth hour; (Luke 23:44) The light of the sun went out, and the dividing screen (curtain) in the dwelling (Temple) was parted in two. (Luke 23:45) And Existing-One-is-salvation gave a loud cry and said, Father, into your hands I give my spirit: and when he had said this, he gave up his spirit. (Luke 23:46) Now there was a man named He-shall-add (Joseph), a man of authority and a good and upright man (Luke 23:50) of A-height (Arimathaea), a town of the Praised-ones (the Jews), (Luke 23:51) And he took it down, and folding it in a linen cloth, he put it in a place cut in the rock for a dead body; and no one had ever been put in it. (Luke 23:53)

The meanings used:

  • Curtain (katapetasma, G2665): "something spread thoroughly, i.e. (specially) the door screen (to the Most Holy Place) in the Jewish Temple". It is used six times in the New Testament: Matthew 27:51, Mark 15:38, Luke 23:45, Hebrews 6:19, 9:3 and 10:20. In the Greek Old Testament it is the word used for the Hebrew paroket (H6532) and H4539.
  • Screen (paroket, H6532): "a separatrix, i.e. (the sacred) screen". It is the veil of Exodus 26:31 and 2 Chronicles 3:14. The BBE says "veil" there and "curtain" in the gospels.
  • Parted (schizo, G4977): "to split or sever (literally or figuratively)". Thayer's: "to cleave, cleave asunder, rend". It has 11 occurrences in 10 verses. It is the word in Luke 23:45, Matthew 27:51 (the curtain and the rocks) and Mark 15:38. It is also the word in Mark 1:10, "the heavens broken open" (BBE). John uses it twice, in 19:24 and 21:11, and both times for something that is not parted (see "Why John has no curtain").
  • Divide (badal, H914): the word the KJV renders "divide" and "separate". Its list has 42 occurrences, including Genesis 1:4, 1:6, 1:7, 1:14 and 1:18, and Exodus 26:33. Only the occurrence list is used here, not a Strong's definition.
  • Winged ones (kerub, H3742): "of uncertain derivation; a cherub or imaginary figure". The BBE never says "cherubim", it says "winged ones" or "winged beings". The Blue Letter Bible list for this word includes the guards of Eden (Genesis 3:24), the mercy-seat figures (Exodus 25:18-20, 37:7-8), 1 Kings 6:23-35 and 2 Chronicles 3:7 and 3:10-14.
  • Paradise (paradeisos, G3857): "of Oriental origin (compare H6508); a park, i.e. (specially), an Eden (place of future happiness, 'paradise')". In the New Testament it is used three times only: Luke 23:43, 2 Corinthians 12:4 and Revelation 2:7. The Greek Old Testament uses it for the garden of Eden throughout Genesis 2 and 3 (2:8 to 3:24 are on the list). Revelation 2:7 has "the tree of life ... in the midst of the paradise of God".
  • Solomon's covered way (stoa, G4745): "a colonnade or interior piazza". The KJV says "porch". It is used in John 5:2, John 10:23, Acts 3:11 and Acts 5:12, and Solomon is named in the last three.
  • Temple (naos, G3485): "from a primary naio (to dwell); a fane, shrine, temple". Thayer's says it is the sanctuary itself, the holy place and the most holy, and it is a different word from hieron, the whole temple area. John uses naos only in 2:19-21, and there the temple is Jesus' body.
  • Failed (ekleipo, G1587): "to fail, die out, come to an end", as the BibleHub interlinear lists it. Luke 23:45 reads "the sun failing" in the Greek; the BBE gives "went out". Matthew and Mark do not have this clause.
  • Midst (mesos, G3319): the middle. Luke's Greek says the curtain parted "in the midst". The BBE does not say "in the middle" in any of the three gospels. It says "in two".
  • Arimathaea (G707): "of Hebrew origin (H7414); Arimathaea (or Ramah)". Thayer's gives "a height". That gloss is Thayer's, not Strong's own.
  • Joseph (G2501): Strong's links it to the Hebrew Yoseph (H3130), "let him add (or perhaps simply active participle adding)", from yasaph (H3254), "more, again, add, increase". Blue Letter Bible's outline glosses it "Jehovah has added". In this course: he shall add.
  • Jews (Ioudaios, G2453): "Judaean, i.e. belonging to Jehudah". Jehudah (H3063) is "celebrated" in Strong's, from yadah (H3034), "praise" and "give thanks", and Blue Letter Bible's outline glosses it "praised". In this course the Jews are the Praised-ones.
  • Jesus (G2424): the Greek form of Yehoshua (H3091), from H3068 (YHVH, the Existing One) and H3467 (yasha, "to save"). In this course: Existing One is salvation.
  • Luke (G3065): "contracted from Latin Lucanus" in Strong's. The name gloss (Thayer's, on Blue Letter Bible) is "light-giving". Matthew (G3156): Blue Letter Bible gives "gift of Jehovah", so here "gift of the Existing One". Mark (G3138): "of Latin origin; Marcus", and the Thayer's gloss is "a defense". John (G2491): "of Hebrew origin (Yochanan, H3110)", and the usual outline gloss is "Jehovah is a gracious giver", so here "Existing One is gracious".

Who is in the scene

The text names the one who is promised a garden, a land that goes dark, a sun that fails, a curtain in the temple, a spirit that is given up, a man from a height with a tomb nobody has been put in, and a captain who says what he saw. In this course's reading, where the many voices are gathered under one present awareness (see the court language), all of them are in you.

This is how the Bible is set up, not an extra layer on top of it. Genesis 1:26 makes man the one made in the judges' own image (Lesson 6), so every scene has the same three parts: you, the identity you claim, and the judges that make it real. Here the identity gives up its spirit, the edge between the two states is parted, and a new ground is cut that nobody has been in.

How to read it, step by step:

  1. The garden is named first. Luke 23:43 has "Today you will be with me in Paradise". The Greek word, paradeisos (G3857), is the one the Greek Old Testament uses for Eden all through Genesis 2 and 3, as in "the Lord God made a garden in the east, in Eden" (Genesis 2:8, BBE). Of the three gospels that tell the curtain, only Luke has this line, and in the New Testament the word is used only here, in 2 Corinthians 12:4 and in Revelation 2:7. So in Luke the ground is named before anything is parted, as Eden was (Lesson 5).
  2. The land goes dark and the light fails. "all the land was dark till the ninth hour" and "the light of the sun went out" (Luke 23:44-45). Genesis 1 starts with darkness, and on the fourth day the lights are set in the arch: "And God put them in the arch of heaven, to give light on the earth" (Genesis 1:17, BBE). In Luke those set lights are the thing that fails, and only Luke says so. The Greek is "the sun failing" (G1587). The arch is where the lights were put, and the next thing in Luke 23:45 is the curtain.
  3. The curtain does the arch's work. Genesis 1:6 has "Let there be a solid arch stretching over the waters, parting the waters from the waters", and 1:7 "a division between the waters which were under the arch and those which were over it". Exodus 26:33 has "the veil is to be a division between the holy place and the most holy" (BBE). All three use the Hebrew badal (H914), and all three verses are on its list of 42 occurrences. That is a word link, not only a picture. Genesis 1:8 names the arch Heaven, and in Lesson 4 day two is the edge between where you are now and where you are heading. The curtain is the same job set in a room: it keeps one side apart from the other. The Greek for it, katapetasma, is the word used for the Hebrew screen (paroket, "a separatrix").
  4. The winged ones are on the curtain. "And you are to make a veil of the best linen, blue and purple and red, worked with designs of winged ones by a good workman" (Exodus 26:31, BBE), and "he made the veil of blue and purple and red, of the best linen, worked with winged ones" (2 Chronicles 3:14, BBE). Genesis 3:24 has "So he sent the man out; and at the east of the garden of Eden he put winged ones and a flaming sword turning every way to keep the way to the tree of life" (BBE). The Hebrew kerub (H3742) is on the list for Genesis 3:24 and for 2 Chronicles 3:14. In Exodus 26:31 and 36:35 the BBE says "winged ones" too, so for those two verses the link is the BBE's wording, not a word link. What stood at the edge of Eden to keep the way is worked into the screen that stands at the edge of the most holy place.
  5. Solomon's temple is a garden in a room. "In the inmost room he made two winged beings of olive-wood, ten cubits high" (1 Kings 6:23, BBE). "And all the walls of the house inside and out were ornamented with forms of winged ones and palm-trees and open flowers" (1 Kings 6:29, BBE), and the doors are the same (6:32, 6:35). So the house is carved with winged ones, trees and flowers: the Eden picture, with the guards in it. Luke says "the Temple", naos (G3485), which Thayer's gives as the sanctuary itself. Luke does not name Solomon, but John does place him in it: "Then came the feast of the opening of the Temple in Jerusalem: it was winter; And Jesus was walking in the Temple, in Solomon's covered way" (John 10:22-23, BBE). The word for the covered way is stoa (G4745), "a colonnade or interior piazza", and Thayer's calls Solomon's porch a portico built by Solomon in the eastern part of the temple. It is used four times in the New Testament: John 5:2, John 10:23, Acts 3:11 and Acts 5:12. Three of those four name Solomon, and the gospel one is John 10:23, so the gospels do hint at Solomon's house. The east is also where the winged ones were put at Eden (Genesis 3:24), but the text does not say that the portico is that east, so that is a matching picture.
  6. The curtain is parted. The word is schizo (G4977), "to split or sever". It is the same word as in Mark 1:10, where Jesus sees "the heavens broken open" (BBE). Heaven is what the arch is named in Genesis 1:8, so the same Greek word is used when the heavens are broken at the start of Mark and when the curtain is parted at the end of it (15:38). In this reading the edge that keeps the two sides apart is opened, and the way that Genesis 3:24 shut is no longer kept by the screen. Luke's Greek says it parts "in the midst". The BBE says "in two". Matthew and Mark add "from end to end" (Matthew 27:51, Mark 15:38, BBE), so the parting runs the whole length.
  7. Then the spirit is given up, or first. In Luke the order is curtain, then "Father, into your hands I give my spirit" (23:45-46). In Matthew and Mark the order is the other way round: "And Jesus gave another loud cry, and gave up his spirit. And the curtain of the Temple was parted in two from end to end" (Matthew 27:50-51, BBE), and "And Jesus gave a loud cry, and gave up his spirit. And the curtain of the Temple was parted in two from end to end" (Mark 15:37-38, BBE). In Genesis 2:7 the Lord God made man "breathing into him the breath of life" (BBE). The spirit given up here is a matching picture of that breath, not a word link. Luke shows the edge opened, then the giving. Matthew and Mark show the giving, then the edge opened.
  8. A new ground is cut. After the curtain comes Joseph, "he shall add" (G2501), from Arimathaea, "a height" (G707, Thayer's), a town of the Jews, "the praised" (G2453). He "put it in a place cut in the rock for a dead body; and no one had ever been put in it" (Luke 23:53, BBE). The first ground was named before anyone stood on it, and here a new ground is cut before anyone is put in it.

Lesson 5 says every story is the Genesis 1 picture, changed. Here the pieces of the curtain scene are all in Genesis 1 to 3, and each one is turned.

In Genesis the arch is made on day two and the winged ones are put at the edge after the man is sent out. In the scene at the cross the edge and the guard are in one piece of linen, the light that was set in the arch fails, and the piece is parted. So the order of Genesis 1 to 3 is run back.

Four tellings, one scene

There are four gospels, and three of them tell the curtain. In this course's reading each telling is the reader going through the same scene, and each goes the way its own name says. The curtain is the same in each one. What changes is what stands around it. John is the fourth go at the scene, and it is the one where nothing is torn (see the next section).

GospelThe nameWhat stands around the curtain

The ground itself moves, and the captain says "Truly this was a son of God" (27:54)
Mark"a defense" (G3138)Spirit given up, then the curtain "from end to end" (15:37-38). The same Greek word, schizo, opens the telling with "the heavens broken open" (1:10), so the telling has the word at both ends. The captain says "Truly this man was a son of God" (15:39)

Luke's name says light-giving, and Luke is the telling where the light goes out and a garden is named just before. Matthew keeps the ground in the scene, with the earth-shock and the opened graves. Mark has the one Greek word at both ends of the telling. All four stay, and they are read side by side, not chosen between.

Why John has no curtain

John has no curtain verse. The text does not say why, but his telling gives three things the others do not.

  1. The temple is already the body. The word naos (G3485) is used in John only in 2:19-21. "Send destruction on this Temple and I will put it up again in three days" (John 2:19, BBE), and John says he was speaking of the temple of his body. So by the cross John has already moved the sanctuary into the person, and there is no building left to part. What is done at the cross is done to the body: "one of the men made a wound in his side with a spear" (19:34, BBE), and "No bone of his body will be broken" (19:36, BBE).
  2. Nothing is torn. The word schizo, "parted", is used 11 times in 10 verses in the New Testament. John has it twice, and both times it is for something that holds. The coat: "the coat was without a join, made out of one bit of cloth" and "Let this not be cut up" (John 19:23-24, BBE). The net: "though there was such a number the net was not broken" (John 21:11, BBE), with 153 great fish in it. John does use the related noun schisma (G4978) three times for a crowd divided over Jesus (7:43, 9:16, 10:19), but the verb is kept for what stays whole.
  3. The garden is already there. In Luke a garden is promised, then the edge is opened. In John the garden is there from the arrest ("he went out with his disciples over the stream Kedron to a garden", 18:1, BBE). It is there at the cross ("a garden near the cross, and in the garden a new place for the dead in which no man had ever been put", 19:41, BBE), and Mary takes Jesus for the gardener (20:15). John's cross has no darkness. The dark comes on the morning Mary goes to the tomb ("while it was still dark", 20:1, BBE). So where Luke's reader still has to be let in, John's reader is already inside.

One matching picture sits in the same place as the winged ones. At the tomb Mary "saw two angels in white seated where the body of Jesus had been, one at the head and the other at the feet" (John 20:12, BBE). The Greek word there is angelos, not kerub, so this is a picture, not a word link.

Read as four goes at the same scene, John is the one where it holds. After the cross he keeps going (chapters 20 and 21), and the net comes in full and unbroken. The text does not rank the gospels, so "the one where it holds" is this course's reading, not a verse.

A short note on the words

"In the midst" and "in two". Luke's Greek has the curtain parted "in the midst" (G3319), and the BBE says "in two". Genesis 3:3 has "the tree in the middle of the garden" and Genesis 2:9 puts the tree of life in the middle too. So the middle in Luke 23:45 and the middle of the garden is a matching picture, not a word link.

Three tellings, two orders. The text does not say why Luke puts the parting before the spirit is given up and Matthew and Mark put it after. This page keeps both orders and reads each from the side it is told from, the way the contradiction page reads a clashing pair. It does not choose between them.

What is not settled here. Luke does not name Solomon, and John 10:23 is the gospel verse that does. The winged ones on the veil in Exodus 26:31 and 36:35 are a link by the BBE's wording only. The breath in Genesis 2:7 and the spirit in Luke 23:46 are a matching picture, and so are the two angels in John 20:12. The text does not say why the curtain is parted, and it does not say why John leaves it out. What is given here is the pattern the text does supply.

Where this sits in the storyline

This is the Genesis 3 shut door run in reverse. In Genesis 1 an arch divides, in Genesis 2 a garden is made, and in Genesis 3 the man is sent out and winged ones are put at the east to keep the way to the tree of life. In Luke the garden is promised first, the light that was set in the arch fails, and the curtain that carries the winged ones is parted, with the same word that Genesis 1:6 uses for the arch's dividing now used for what the veil divides. The court's ruling is not in the verse. What the scene shows is the edge between the two states being opened, and in this reading that is the reader coming back to the way that Genesis 3:24 shut. John tells the same scene with the garden already there, the temple already the body, and nothing torn.

Remember: The curtain does the arch's job, and the Hebrew word for its dividing is the one used in Genesis 1:6. It carries the winged ones that Genesis 3:24 puts at the east of Eden to keep the way to the tree of life. In Luke a garden is promised first, the light of the sun fails, and then the curtain is parted. The edge that kept the way shut is opened. John has no curtain: his temple is already the body, his garden is already there, and the coat and the net are not torn.
