What Does "Sacrifice" Mean? Why Things Are Killed in the Bible

And after a time, Gotten brought to the Existing One an offering, a gift of the fruits of the ground. And Breath brought an offering of the young lambs of his flock and of their fat. And the Existing One was pleased with Breath's offering; but in Gotten and his offering he had no pleasure. And Gotten was angry and his face became sad. And the Existing One said to Gotten, Why are you angry? and why is your face sad? If you do well, will you not be lifted up? and if you do wrong, sin (the same word as sin-offering) is crouching at the door, desiring to have you, but do not let it be your master. — Genesis 4:3-7, with the meanings put in

Most people picture a sacrifice as giving something up: an animal is killed and a debt is paid. The Bible does not start there. In the original scene of Genesis 1 nothing is killed, because the food is plants (Genesis 1:29). The first offering in the Bible, in Genesis 4, names no killing either. The first killing named comes in the very next scene, when Cain kills Abel. This page follows the offering from Genesis 4 to Leviticus 1 and asks the same three things at each step: what is brought, who is it brought to, and what does the court say?

How this page reads the Bible. This course reads every story as having the same three parts: you, the identity you claim, and the judges that make it real, because Genesis 1:26 makes man the one made in the judges' own image (Lesson 6). A name tells you what the state is like before the story starts (Lesson 9). The Existing One (YHVH, "the Lord" in the BBE) is present awareness, and Elohim is the Judges (Lesson 3). Each step below is checked against the verse (BBE), Strong's and the Hebrew word by word. If this is your first page, The Big Idea is the place to start.

The short answer: an offering is something of a man's own kind, brought to the door and held up before the face of the court to be seen. The court looks at the man first and the gift second: "If you do well, will you not be lifted up?" (Genesis 4:7). Killing is not in the original scene, and no killing word is used of Abel's offering or Noah's. It comes in after the curse on the ground. Cain kills Abel (4:8). After the flood the court allows flesh as food and fences the blood as life (9:3-5). The Law then brings the killing that was already going on to the door (Leviticus 17:3-5). And in the court the offerings are refused: "I said nothing to your fathers ... about burned offerings" (Jeremiah 7:22). Killing is ordered in the Law (Exodus 12:21, 29:11, Leviticus 1:5), but it is not what wins the verdict. The court values what is brought and the one who brings it, to see what may go into the scene of pleasure. What it asks for is doing well, mercy, and giving ear to its voice.

In six lines:

  1. The original scene has no killing. The food is plants, and the livestock are ruled, not killed (Genesis 1:26, 1:29).
  2. The first offerings name no killing. Cain brings from the ground and Abel brings from the flock, and the court looks at the man first (4:3-7). The first killing is Cain killing Abel, and the blood is heard (4:8-10).
  3. Noah's ascending offerings are followed by the court saying in its heart that it will not again curse the ground (8:20-22). Only after that is flesh given as food, with the blood kept as life (9:3-5).
  4. In Genesis 22 the court's word is "go up". The slaughter word is first used when Abraham lifts the knife, and the court stops the hand. The lamb is given, and the seed is not killed.
  5. The Law does not start the killing. It brings what the people were already killing in the open country to the door, and it says the blood is the life (Leviticus 17:3-5, 17:11).
  6. In the court the offerings are refused (Isaiah 1, Jeremiah 7, Psalm 40, Psalm 50, Hosea 6, Isaiah 66), and what is asked is doing well, hearing the voice, mercy and praise.

The course in short

  • The Bible is read as a courtroom inside the reader (Lesson 1). The Judges (Elohim) rule on the identity the reader holds, and the Existing One, the reader's own present awareness, holds it as I AM (Lesson 2).
  • Every story is the Genesis 1 picture, changed (Lesson 5).
  • Everything comes back after its kind (Lesson 7): what is filed is what is enforced.
  • Names are identity codes (the names lesson), so every name below is read for its meaning.

The Genesis scene this is built from

Lesson 5 says every story is the Genesis 1 picture, changed, and the original scene has four parts: the ground, the seed, the stand and the time. The part of Genesis 1 that the offerings are built from is day six: the livestock, the man, and the food that is given.

And the Judges said, Let the earth give birth to all sorts of living things, livestock and all things moving on the earth, and beasts of the earth after their sort: and it was so. And the Judges made the beast of the earth after its sort, and the livestock after their sort, and everything moving on the face of the earth after its sort: and the Judges saw that it was good. And the Judges said, Let us make man in our image, like us: and let him have rule over the fish of the sea and over the birds of the air and over the livestock and over all the earth and over every living thing which goes flat on the earth. — Genesis 1:24-26, with the meanings put in
And the Judges said, See, I have given you every plant producing seed, on the face of all the earth, and every tree which has fruit producing seed: they will be for your food: — Genesis 1:29, with the meanings put in
  • The ground: "the earth" brings the living things out (1:24).
  • The seed, the kind: each creature comes "after its sort", and the livestock "after their sort" (1:24-25). The word for livestock is behemah.
  • The stand: the man (adam) is made "like us" and set over the livestock to rule them (1:26).
  • The time: the lights are set for the appointed times, moed (1:14).
  • The food: the plants and the fruit-trees are given to the man for food (1:29). Nothing in the scene is killed.
  • The verdict: the Judges see it, and "it was good" (1:25). The verdict comes at once.

So in the original scene the man stands on named ground over the livestock, each kind after its kind, the food is plants, nothing is killed, and the Judges say it is good. Everything below is that scene changed.

The meanings used

  • Offering, gift (minchah, H4503): Strong's "a donation; ... specifically a sacrificial offering (usually bloodless and voluntary)". 211 uses in the KJV count. Its first use is Genesis 4:3, and its second is Jacob's gift to Esau (Genesis 32:13-21). The verb for it in Genesis 4:3 is hevi, "brought" (bo, H935).
  • Something brought near (korban, H7133): Strong's "something brought near the altar, i.e. a sacrificial present", from qarab (H7126), "bring near". 82 uses in 78 verses, and its first use is Leviticus 1:2. The word is not in Genesis at all.
  • Sin, sin-offering (chattat, H2403): one word for both. In Genesis 4:7 it is crouching at the door, and in Leviticus 4:4 the sin-offering is taken "to the door of the Tent of meeting".
  • Lifted up (se'et, H7613), the BBE's "honour"; do well (yatav, H3190), the verb of teitiv in Genesis 4:7 and of heitev in Isaiah 1:17; regard (sha'ah, H8159), the BBE's "was pleased" and "no pleasure" in 4:4-5.
  • Eden (H5731): the same word as H5730, "pleasure". Vow (neder, H5088): "a promise (to God)"; 60 occurrences, the first in Genesis 28:20.
  • Put to death (harag, H2026): the verb of Cain killing Abel (4:8) and of Seth being given "in place of Abel, whom Cain put to death" (4:25). Ground (adamah, H127).
  • Rest (nuach, H5117): Noah (H5146) is "the same as nuach; rest", and the "sweet smell" of Genesis 8:21 is nichoach (H5207), from the same root. The dove found no "resting-place" in 8:9 (BBE).
  • Livestock (behemah, H929): the word of Genesis 1:24 and 1:26, and of "clean" livestock in 8:20. Cause to go up (alah, H5927) and ascending offering (olah, H5930): the words of Genesis 8:20 and 22:2.
  • Slaughter (shachat, H7819): first used in Genesis 22:10, then Genesis 37:31 and Exodus 12:6. Slaughter for sacrifice (zabach, H2076): BDB "slaughter for sacrifice"; first used in Genesis 31:54, then 46:1 and Exodus 3:18.
  • Cover (kaphar, H3722): Strong's "to cover (specifically with bitumen)". Its first use is the pitch on the ark (Genesis 6:14), and its second is Jacob covering Esau's face with the gift (Genesis 32:20). Accept (ratsah, H7521): the verb of venirtsah in Leviticus 1:4 and of vattirtseni, "you accepted me", in Genesis 33:10.
  • Deep sleep (tardemah, H8639): 7 uses. The first is the Man's sleep in Genesis 2:21 and the second is Father-of-many's sleep in Genesis 15:12.
  • Appointed time (moed, H4150): first used in Genesis 1:14, and the "Tent of the Appointed Time" of Leviticus 1:1.
  • The names: Cain (H7014) is "Gotten", from Eve's words in 4:1, "I have gotten" (KJV), and Strong's has "a play upon" qanah (H7069); BDB and Blue Letter Bible also give "possession". Abel (H1893) is "Breath". Noah (H5146) is "Rest". Abraham is "Father of many". Isaac (H3327) is "Laughter". Moriah (H4179) is "seen of Jah", written here "Seen-of-the-Existing-One". Israel (H3478) is "He-prevails". Aaron is "Light-bringer", as on the Aaron page.
  • YHVH (H3068): the Existing One, the BBE's "the Lord". Elohim (H430): the Judges.

Step by step, from Genesis 1 to the court

  1. Nothing is killed in the original scene (Genesis 1:24-29). The livestock come "after their sort" and the man is set over them to rule (1:24-26). The food is plants: "every plant producing seed ... and every tree which has fruit" (1:29). The same green plants go to the beasts and birds as their food, so no one in the scene eats anyone.
  2. The curse, and the skins (Genesis 3:17, 21). The ground is "cursed on your account" (3:17), and the man is sent out "to be a worker on the earth" (3:23, BBE). Then the Existing One, the Judges, "made ... coats of skins" (3:21). The verse says "made". It does not say that anything was killed, or how, or from what, and so the killing is not in Genesis 3 as the text gives it (the fig leaves and skins page reads the skins themselves).
  3. The first offerings (Genesis 4:3-7). The man now works the ground (4:2), and Gotten brings "the fruits of the ground" while Breath brings "the young lambs of his flock and of their fat", which is the livestock of day six, the part of the original scene the man still rules. The Hebrew verb for both is hevi, "brought", and the word is minchah, "gift", which Strong's calls "usually bloodless and voluntary". Neither verse names killing or burning, though Abel's gift of "their fat" suggests animals were killed. The court looks at the man first: it "was pleased with Breath's offering" and had "no pleasure" in Gotten and his offering (4:4-5). Then it asks Gotten, not his gift, the question: "If you do well, will you not be lifted up? and if you do wrong, sin is waiting at the door" (4:7). The ground Gotten brings from is the ground of 3:17, already cursed (see Lesson 17), and the name says what he does: he is "Gotten", and he takes. Breath is the small start of day six, and he is gone at once.
  4. The first killing (Genesis 4:8-11, 4:16, 4:25). Gotten attacks Breath in the field and "put him to death" (harag). The first blood in the Bible is a killed man's, and it is heard: "the voice of your brother's blood is crying to me from the ground" (4:10). The ground that was cursed now has "its mouth open to take your brother's blood from your hand" (4:11). Gotten goes out "from before the face of the Existing One" (4:16), the face before which every offering of Leviticus is brought (1:3, 1:5). In 4:25 Seth is "another seed in place of Abel", the new seed after its kind.
  5. Noah, Rest, and the aroma of rest (Genesis 5:29, 8:20-22). Rest is named for the rest he will give "because of the ground which was cursed" (5:29). Out of the ark he makes an altar, and from every clean livestock and bird he "made burned offerings" (8:20). The Hebrew is vayal olot, "he caused to go up ascending offerings", from alah, and the livestock are the behemah of Genesis 1:24 and 1:26. The text has no slaughter verb. What comes up is reach hannichoach, "an aroma of rest", from nuach, the root of Noah's name and of the dove's "resting-place" (8:9). The court then says "in his heart": "I will not again put a curse on the ground because of man" (8:21). That answers the curse of 3:17 and the name of 5:29. The reason given is that "the thoughts of man's heart are evil from his earliest days", and the ground is still not cursed again.
  6. Flesh as food, and the blood kept (Genesis 9:3-5). Straight after, for the first time in the Bible, the court gives flesh as food: "Every living and moving thing will be food for you; I give them all to you as before I gave you all green things" (9:3). "As before" points back to the plants of 1:29. In the same breath the blood is fenced: "flesh with the life-blood in it you may not take for food" (9:4), and "for your blood, which is your life, will I take payment ... for the blood of his brother-man" (9:5), which is Gotten and Breath again. The same verse asks for it "from every beast" as well as from every man, and the Hebrew of 9:4 is basar benafsho damo, "flesh in its life, its blood". The creatures that are nephesh in Genesis 1 and 2 are asked for the blood as the man is. So the Bible itself marks the change from the original scene: flesh as food comes in after the curse and after the flood, and the blood is kept as the life.
  7. The agreement while the man sleeps (Genesis 15:12-18). "A deep sleep came on Father-of-many" (15:12). The Hebrew word, tardemah, is the word of the Man's sleep in Genesis 2:21, and these are the first two of its seven uses. Then "a smoking fire and a flaming light ... went between the parts of the bodies", and "in that day the Existing One made an agreement" (15:17-18). The text names the passing fire, and the one who sleeps is not the one who passes. Psalm 50:5 later calls the same thing "an agreement with me by offerings".
  8. Father-of-many and Laughter (Genesis 22). The court's word is vehaalehu, "cause him to go up", the verb of Noah's offerings (22:2), on a mountain in the land of Seen-of-the-Existing-One. On the way, "the Judges themselves will give the lamb for the ascending offering" (22:8). The slaughter word, shachat, is first used in the Bible when Father-of-many "took the knife to slay" (22:10). The next words are the court's: "Let not your hand be stretched out against the boy to do anything to him" (22:12). The sheep fixed by its horns in the brushwood is offered "in place of his son" (22:13). The seed of many, Laughter, is not killed, the lamb is given by the Judges, and the Hebrew place-name is "the Existing One sees".
  9. The same words between two men (Genesis 32-33). Before the Law has a word for it, Jacob meets his brother with a minchah, a gift, and says he will "cover his face" with it (32:20, kaphar, the second use of the word, after the pitch on the ark). When they meet he says, "accept my gift from my hand", and "I have seen your face as one sees the face of the Judges", and "you accepted me" (33:10, ratsah). The gift, the face, the covering and the acceptance of Leviticus 1:3-4 are already in a meeting between two people.
  10. The Passover, the first slaughter in the Law (Exodus 12). The Passover is the third use of shachat in the Bible (12:6), after Father-of-many's knife and the goat killed to deceive Jacob (37:31). A lamb is taken "for every family" (12:3), the blood is a "sign on the houses where you are", and the court says, "when I see the blood I will go over you" (12:13). The lamb is eaten inside the house by people "dressed as if for a journey" (12:11). The court's seeing is again the verdict: it looks at what is on the house.
  11. The Law brings the killing to the door (Leviticus 17:3-5, 11). Leviticus 17 gives its own reason: the people were already putting animals to death "in the open country", and the order is to take them "to the door of the Tent of meeting" instead (17:5). A man who kills and does not bring it "has taken life", and "its blood will be on him" (17:4). That is the rule of Genesis 9:4-5, with the blood as life, brought to a door. And 17:11 says why: "the life of the flesh is in its blood". The Hebrew for what the blood does, kaphar, "to cover", is the word of the pitch on the ark and of Jacob covering Esau's face.
  12. Leviticus 1: man brings near something brought near (1:2-5). Adam ki yaqriv mikkem korban: the man is adam, the verb hiqriv is "bring near", and the thing is korban, "something brought near", from the same root. The offering is from the livestock, brought "at the door of the Tent of the Appointed Time", "before the face of the Existing One", with the hand on its head. The verdict is the one asked for in Genesis 4: venirtsah lo lekapper alav, "and it shall be accepted for him, to cover over him". The BBE's "to take away his sin" has no word for sin in it. And the priests "bring near the blood", the verb of 1:2 again.
  13. Brought near, but not what was ordered (Leviticus 10:1). Light-bringer's sons took their vessels and put fire in them, and the BBE has them "burning strange fire before the Lord, which he had not given them orders to do". The Hebrew verb is vayyaqrivu, "they brought near", the verb of Leviticus 1:2 and 1:5, and the clause is asher lo tsivvah otam, "which he had not ordered them". The place is the same, before the face. What differs is what was brought.
  14. In the court: "I take no pleasure in the blood" (Isaiah 1:11-17). The coming is the coming before the face, and the offerings are refused: "I take no pleasure in the blood of oxen, or of lambs, or of he-goats" (1:11). Then: "your hands are full of blood" (1:15), which is Gotten's "blood from your hand" (4:11). And the way out is the verb of Genesis 4:7. Heitev, "take pleasure in well-doing" (1:17), is from yatav, the root of teitiv, "if you do well". Isaiah 1 is Genesis 4 again: an offering, a face, blood on the hands, and "do well".
  15. In the court: "I said nothing about burned offerings" (Jeremiah 7:21-23). The court tells the people to put their burned offerings with their offerings of beasts "and take flesh for your food" (7:21), which is the permission of Genesis 9:3 turned back on them. Then: "I said nothing to your fathers, and gave them no orders, on the day when I took them out of Egypt, about burned offerings or offerings of beasts" (7:22). The order of that day was "Give ear to my voice" (7:23). The Passover killing was ordered in Egypt (Exodus 12:21), so this cannot mean that no killing was ever ordered. In this reading the weight falls on 7:23. Leviticus 17:5 gives the Law's own reason: what the people were already killing is brought to the door. The order underneath is the one Jeremiah names.
  16. In the court: Psalm 50, Psalm 40, Hosea 6, Isaiah 66. "Let my saints come together to me; those who have made an agreement with me by offerings" (Psalm 50:5, the agreement of Genesis 15). The court says it will not take up a cause over the offerings, and "every beast of the woodland is mine, and the cattle on a thousand hills" (50:8, 50:10), so the livestock of Genesis 1 were never the man's to give. "Am I to take the flesh of the ox for my food?" (50:13), and what is asked is an offering of thanksgiving (50:14). Psalm 40:6 says "ears you made for me" in place of offerings and burned offerings. Hosea 6:6 sets "mercy" and "the knowledge of the Judges" above them. And Isaiah 66:3 says it as plainly as the Bible does: "He who puts an ox to death puts a man to death." That is Genesis 4:8 and 4:10, and it is the court's own view of the killing.

In this reading the offerings are the way of coming near again, back towards the scene of pleasure. The winged ones on the veil recall the "winged ones" set at the east of Eden (Genesis 3:24).

Court language, vows and laws

The story of the offering is told in the language of a court. The agreement is made with spoken words, the people answer that they will do them, they are written and read, and blood marks the agreement (Exodus 24:3-8). The book of the law is to be "a witness against you", and heaven and earth are called as witnesses (Deuteronomy 31:26, 28).

So when the case is opened, the witnesses are called. "Give ear, O heavens, and you, O earth" (Isaiah 1:2). "His voice will go out to the heavens and to the earth, for the judging of his people" (Psalm 50:4). Micah calls the mountains to hear the Lord's cause, and asks whether burned offerings will win favour; the answer is doing right, loving mercy and walking humbly (Micah 6:1-8).

Vows are part of this. Where the BBE has "keep the agreements" in Psalm 50:14, the Hebrew word is neder (H5088), a vow. The question is what you are putting into the creation scene, and whether you stand by it.

Valued by the court: what may go into the scene

In Genesis 1 the Judges look at each thing and call it good. In Leviticus there are many identities, and mixed ones, so the looking is spelled out case by case.

  • Sort. "Make a division between the holy and the common, and between the unclean and the clean" (10:10).
  • Value. "Whatever value the priest puts on it, so will it be" (27:12).
  • No swapping. "It may not be changed in any way, a good given for a bad, or a bad for a good" (27:10).
  • Refuse. "Anything which has a mark you may not give; it will not make you pleasing to the Lord" (22:20).

The animal that is killed is the one without a mark, so in this reading the killing is not a way of getting rid of what is wrong. It is the point of no taking back: you stop holding the state as your own, and its life, the blood, is given to the court (17:11). The ascending offering then goes up whole.

One flesh: holding the value as real

"For this cause will a man go away from his father and his mother and be joined to his wife; and they will be one flesh" (Genesis 2:24). Read as a pattern of the mind, this is to leave one state and be joined to another, holding its value as real until it is not outside you.

The Hebrew for "flesh" in "one flesh" is basar (H1320), and it is the word of Genesis 9:4: "flesh with the life-blood in it you may not take for food". There the Hebrew has three words side by side: flesh (basar), its life (nephesh), its blood (dam). Deuteronomy 12:23 puts them in one verse: the blood is the life, and you may not eat the life with the flesh. Leviticus 17:11 has them too: the life of the flesh is in the blood. In this reading you may take a state in until it is one flesh with you, but its live part, the blood, is not yours to take as food. The Blood page follows this word by word.

Some offerings are eaten. The flesh of the praise offering is eaten the same day, and none is kept till the morning (Leviticus 7:15). Eaten on the third day, it "will not be put to the account of him who gives it" (7:18). In this reading the union is held while it is live. Not everything is taken in: the ascending offering goes up whole, and the blood is not eaten. This is why the sorting is so strict: what you become one with brings forth after its kind.

Why is anything killed?

The text gives four answers, in order.

  1. Not because the original scene asks for it. The food of Genesis 1 is plants, the livestock are ruled, and the Judges say "good" at once.
  2. Because the man has left that scene. The ground is cursed (3:17), he is a worker on it (3:23), and the first killing is Gotten's (4:8). After the flood the court gives flesh as food (9:3) and guards the blood as life (9:4-5). The killing is what the world after the curse does with the livestock. The Blood page follows the blood and the eating of flesh word by word.
  3. The Law does not begin it, and it brings it before the face. The people were already killing in the open country. The Law takes it to the door, calls the blood the life, and calls killing away from the door "taking life" (Leviticus 17:3-5, 11).
  4. The court never wants it on its own. Abel's offering and Noah's are brought, and the first slaughter word is stopped at the hand (Genesis 22:10-12). The court says it said nothing about burned offerings (Jeremiah 7:22), that it does not eat (Psalm 50:13), that it takes no pleasure in the blood (Isaiah 1:11), and that killing an ox is like killing a man (Isaiah 66:3). What it asks for is "do well" (Genesis 4:7, Isaiah 1:17), mercy (Hosea 6:6) and giving ear to its voice (Jeremiah 7:23).

What changed

The cast is the same: the man, the livestock, the ground, a time, and the Judges who give the verdict. Four things change.

  1. The ground. It is cursed (3:17), and everything that follows is brought from that ground or off it.
  2. The man's work. In Genesis 1 he rules the livestock. After the curse he works the ground, brings from it, and kills (4:2-8).
  3. The food. In Genesis 1 it is plants. After the flood flesh is given, and the blood is kept as the life (9:3-5).
  4. The verdict. In Genesis 1 the Judges say "good" as soon as they see. After the curse the verdict is asked for, and it looks at the man before the gift: "If you do well" (4:7). It is "accepted" (Leviticus 1:4) or "I will not accept", and "I take no pleasure in the blood" (Isaiah 1:11).

In this course's reading the man is you, the livestock are the states of a kind that you rule, and the killing is what the man does with them once he has left the original scene. The Law puts that killing before the face of the court, at a door, and the court says the killing is not what it is looking for.

Which part moved?

In the course's reading what moved is the ground. In Genesis 1 the ground gives birth to the livestock and the man rules them. In Genesis 3 it is cursed, and the man is sent out to work it. Everything that is brought, and everything that is killed, comes after that. Three questions can be asked of the scene.

  1. What is the ground, and what does its name mean? The ground of 3:17, the ground that opens its mouth (4:11), the ground that is not cursed again (8:21), and later a door, the Tent of the Appointed Time.
  2. Who is standing on it, and what do their names mean? Gotten, who takes; Breath, who is gone at once; Rest, whose aroma of rest lifts the curse; Father-of-many and Laughter, where the seed is spared; Light-bringer and his sons at the door.
  3. What has changed from the Genesis 1 picture? The man who ruled the livestock now works the cursed ground, brings from it and kills, and the Judges' "good" has become "lifted up", "accepted", or "I take no pleasure".

What the names add

Gotten brings from the ground he works and, when the verdict goes against him, takes his brother's life. Breath is gone like breath, and the voice of his blood is what remains. Rest is the one whose offering brings the aroma of rest, and the curse that his name was given against is not renewed. Father-of-many is asked to bring up Laughter, the seed of many, and the seed comes back with a lamb the Judges give, at a place named Seen-of-the-Existing-One where "the Existing One sees". Light-bringer carries the blood and the fire, and his sons Generous and He-is-my-father bring near fire that was not ordered, as on the Aaron page. He-prevails is the people to whom Leviticus 17 is given and Jeremiah 7 is spoken. The names say who is standing there, and the story goes the way they say.

What it means for the reader

In this reading, to bring an offering is to bring a state of a kind that you hold to the door, before the face of your own present awareness, to be seen by the court. The first thing the court looks at is you: "If you do well, will you not be lifted up?" Gotten brought from the cursed ground and was angry at the verdict, and "sin is waiting at the door, desiring to have you, but do not let it be your master." The verdict is not about the size of the gift or the death of the animal. It is about what you are doing as you bring it.

The Law orders killing, but in the court the killing by itself counts for nothing. It says it gave no orders about burned offerings on the day of the coming out, that it takes no pleasure in blood, that every beast is already its own, and that the one who puts an ox to death is like the one who puts a man to death. What it asks for is the other side: doing well (Isaiah 1:17), mercy and the knowledge of the Judges (Hosea 6:6), thanksgiving (Psalm 50:14), and giving ear to its voice (Jeremiah 7:23). The next time you meet an offering in the Bible, ask four things. What is brought? To whom, and at what door? What does the court say about the one who brought it? And what is being put into the scene?

The offering, then, is a state brought before the court to be valued, to see whether it may go into the scene of pleasure. The killing is one step inside that, and never the point by itself.

Remember: in the original scene nothing is killed and the food is plants. The first offerings, Abel's and Noah's, are brought, and the text uses no killing verb. The first killing is Cain's, and flesh as food comes in after the curse and the flood. The Law brings what was already being killed to the door, and in the court the offerings are refused. English "sacrifice" mostly translates zebach, the slaughter, but the offering of Genesis 4 is a minchah, a gift, and the Law's word korban means something brought near. What the court looks at first is the one who brings.
