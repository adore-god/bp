Jacob, Leah and Rachel: Why Did Jacob Marry Two Sisters? (Genesis 29-35)

Short answer: Jacob went to Laban for one wife and ended with two. In this course the two sisters are two identities held at once. Rachel is the one he can picture, "fair in face and form". Leah is the older one, "weary", joined to him first, in the evening, before he can see her. Then the Existing One sees the one nobody loved, and her sons come first. The loved one waits, envies, and is put to rest on the road. Every turn follows the meaning of a name. At the end the two sisters are named together as the builders of one house (Ruth 4:11).

People search this as "why did Jacob marry Leah and Rachel", "why did Laban trick Jacob", "why was Leah unloved", "what do the names of Jacob's sons mean", "why did Rachel steal the idols" and "how did Rachel die". The story runs across Genesis 29 to 35, so this page takes it one turn at a time.

New here? The course in four lines.

  • Every story has three parts: someone present (the Existing One, which is you, aware right now), the identity they claim (I AM), and the judges who make it real (Elohim). Lesson 1 and Lesson 2.
  • Rule One, after its kind: what is planted gives back only what it is. Lesson 7.
  • Rule Two, leave and join: step away from the old state and join the new one (Genesis 2:24). Lesson 8.
  • Names and places are codes: the meaning tells you what the state is like before the story starts. Lesson 9.

Where this sits in the story

Jacob has been sent to his mother's brother's house to find a wife (28:2). At the well he meets Rachel, kisses her, and Laban takes him in with "you are my bone and my flesh" (29:11, 29:14, BBE). The "she is my sister" page reads the three patriarchs' marriages side by side. This page stays with Jacob, Leah and Rachel, from the well to Rachel's resting-place.

Who is who

In this course the whole scene happens inside you, in the court of Lesson 1. The sisters and the servants are identities, not outside people.

  • Jacob, "heel-catcher" (29:18). The one who claims, the one who grabs a place. He is renamed Israel, "he will prevail", in 35:10.
  • Laban, "white" (29:16). The old family state he was sent back to, with its own rules (older first, a price for the daughters) and its own gods.
  • Leah, "weary" (29:16-17). The older identity, tired and plain to look at, joined first, in the dark.
  • Rachel, "ewe", a good traveller (29:16-17). The identity you can picture, the one who looks right and the one you work for.
  • Bilhah, "timid", and Zilpah, "a trickling" (30:3, 30:9). Identities set under each wife to bear for her.
  • The sons. What each identity brings forth. Each name says what it is (see the list of sons below).
  • "The Lord" (29:31-35, 30:24). The Existing One: you, aware right now, who sees and hears (Lesson 2).
  • "God" in the BBE (30:2-23, 31:24, 35:1-15). The Judges (Elohim): the court that judges, pays, gives and makes real.
  • The images of her father's gods (31:19). The old family state's own judges, still carried by the identity that has just said leave.
  • The heap of witness (31:44-49). The agreement set between the old state and the new one, with the court as witness.
  • Beth-el, "house of El", Ephrath, "fruitfulness", Beth-lehem, "house of bread" (35:1, 35:16-19). The place the new name is given, and the place the traveller is put to rest.

The story, step by step

Each step gives what the text says, then what it means in this course.

1. Two daughters, and the one he can see (29:16-20)

The text: Laban has two daughters. The older is Leah, Weary, and the BBE says her eyes were "clouded". The Hebrew is rak (H7390), "tender; by implication, weak". The younger is Rachel, Ewe, "fair in face and form". The word for form is to'ar (H8389, "figure or appearance"). The word for face is mar'eh (H4758, "a view; also an appearance"), and it comes from ra'ah, "to see". Then: "Jacob was in love with Rachel" (29:18). Seven years of work for her "seemed to him only a very little time" (29:20).

What it means: Jacob loves what he can see. He works seven years for an appearance, and the time feels short because he is looking at the picture. Leah has nothing to look at, so there is nothing in her for him to love. In Lesson 1 the court makes real what is actually offered, and what he is offering is a look.

A note on "sister": the two women are sisters to each other, not to Jacob ("envy of her sister", 30:1). The "my sister, my bride" pair, where the sister is the wife, is in the Song of Solomon (4:9, 4:12).

2. Evening, then morning (29:23-30)

The text: "In the evening he took Leah... in the morning Jacob saw that it was Leah" (29:23, 29:25). Evening comes first and morning second, the same order as every day in Genesis 1: "there was evening and there was morning". Jacob asks "why have you been false to me?" and Laban answers with the old family rule: "In our country we do not let the younger daughter be married before the older" (29:26). Rachel is given after the bride-week, and seven more years follow (29:27-30). Jacob later counts it as "fourteen years because of your daughters" (31:41).

What it means: He offered the picture, but what he joined in the dark was the older state, and in the morning light that is what is there. The heel-catcher, named for grabbing a place, is handed a place he did not pick. This is Rule One (Lesson 7): what comes out of the night is the same kind as the night, the weary state he was already in. The picture is added after, with more work. You pass through what you are before you reach what you picture.

3. The Existing One sees the one nobody loved (29:31-35)

The text: The BBE says Leah "was not loved". The Hebrew is sane (H8130), "to hate", and the KJV has "hated". The Lord sees it (ra'ah, H7200) and gives her a child, while Rachel has none. Her four sons are:

  • Reuben, "see ye a son" (from ra'ah and ben): "The Lord has seen my sorrow."
  • Simeon, "hearing": "it has come to the Lord's ears".
  • Levi, "attached" (from lavah): "Now at last my husband will be united to me".
  • Judah, "celebrated", Praise: "This time I will give praise to the Lord."

Then: "after this she had no more children for a time" (29:35).

What it means: Jacob saw by appearance (mar'eh). The Existing One, you aware right now, sees by the same root what has no appearance: the state you are actually in. Seeing it is what opens it. The four names are a path. First the state is seen. Then it is heard, which means named and owned. Then it is joined to, which is Rule Two (Lesson 8), though the verb here is lavah and not the dabaq of Genesis 2:24. Then it is praised. Her asking moves from the husband's love to praise of the Existing One. She stops needing the man's approval to be fruitful, and the pause that follows is what comes after praise. For Judah's line see the Judah and Tamar page.

4. The contest, and Jacob's answer (30:1-13)

The text: Rachel is "full of envy of her sister" and says "If you do not give me children I will not go on living" (30:1). Jacob answers: "Am I in the place of God, who has kept your body from having fruit?" (30:2). Rachel then says "Here is my servant Bilhah... so that she may have a child on my knees, and I may have a family by her" (30:3). "Have a family" is banah (H1129), "to build", which in Strong's also means "obtain children". It is the verb of Genesis 2:22, where the woman is built. The servants' sons are Dan, "judge" ("God has been my judge"), Naphtali, "my wrestling" ("I have had a great fight with my sister, and I have overcome her"), and, through Leah's servant, Gad, "troop", and Asher, "happy".

What it means: When the picture sees the other state bearing, it turns to envy and tries to force the fruit. "Give me children or I do not live" puts the job on the man. Jacob's answer is that the man is not the Judges. Fruit comes from the court, after its kind, not from the one who claims. So Rachel builds through a second identity set under her own, Bilhah, "timid", with the child "on my knees". The fruit is named judge and wrestling, a fight won over her sister, and it stays on a servant's knees and not in her own body. Leah copies it with Zilpah, and her sons are named troop and happy. Two states competing can only bring forth competing names.

5. The love-fruits (30:14-21)

The text: Reuben finds "love-fruits" in the field "at the time of the grain-cutting" (30:14). The word is dudaim (H1736), "mandrake (as an aphrodisiac)". Leah says "Is it a small thing that you have taken my husband from me?" and Rachel trades the night: "You may have him tonight in exchange for your son's love-fruits" (30:15). Leah tells Jacob "I have given my son's love-fruits as a price for you" (30:16). The same word is in Song of Solomon 7:13 (the Song page). Then come Issachar, "he will bring a reward" ("God has made payment to me"), Zebulun, "habitation" ("God has given me a good bride-price; now at last will I have my husband living with me"), and a daughter, Dinah (30:21).

What it means: The two states now treat the husband as a price. One buys a night with fruit and the other sells it, and each calls him "my husband". The court gives back what was actually held, so the sons come back named payment, reward and bride-price. The fruit being traded is a fruit of the field, a plant that gives seed after its kind (Genesis 1:11). But look where Leah's naming ends: Zebulun, "living with me". What she wants at the end of the trading is not a payment. It is to be settled with the husband, and that is the join of Rule Two. The trade is the long way round to it.

6. Joseph, and the leaving (30:22-25)

The text: "Then God gave thought to Rachel... he made her fertile." She says "God has taken away my shame" and names him Joseph, "let him add": "May the Lord give me another son." Then: "Now after the birth of Joseph, Jacob said to Laban, Let me go away to my place and my country." In Leah's four names (29:32-35) the BBE says "the Lord". From 30:6 to 30:23 it says "God". Rachel's last line, 30:24, goes back to "the Lord".

What it means: In this course "the Lord" is the Existing One and "God" is the Judges (Lesson 2), so the order is the course's order. The Existing One sees and hears (Leah). The Judges judge, pay, give and take away the shame (the second round). Then the Existing One is asked to add. Rachel's fruit comes when she stops wrestling and asks for more, not for a win. The leaving starts only now. The old state can be left once the picture has fruit of its own, because there is then something in the new state that the old state does not hold. (The sticks and the flocks in 30:25-43 are read on the rods and sticks page.)

7. The two women say leave (31:3-19)

The text: The Lord says "Go back to the land of your fathers" (31:3), and Jacob calls "Rachel and Leah" to the field. Both answer together: "What part or heritage is there for us in our father's house?... he took a price for us and now it is all used up" and "whatever God has said to you, do" (31:14-16). Yet "Rachel secretly took the images of the gods of her father's house" (31:19). Laban chases and asks "why have you taken my gods?" (31:30). Jacob, not knowing, says "if anyone of us has them, let him be put to death" (31:32). Laban searches Leah's tent first and Rachel's last, and she sits on the images in the camels' basket (31:33-35). The court speaks to Laban by night: "Take care that you say nothing good or bad to Jacob" (31:24).

What it means: This is Rule Two spoken by the identities themselves. They say the old house has no part for them, and that what it took for them is spent. But the picture still carries the old family's judges, hidden under her. In this course the gods are the Judges, the ones who make a claim real. An identity that leaves while still sitting on the old judges is still filed under the old court, which is why the old state can search the tents and chase. The man does not know she has them, and his own sentence of death falls on the one he loves. The new state is held anyway, because the Judges themselves tell Laban to say nothing to Jacob.

8. The heap of witness (31:43-55)

The text: The old state and the new one make an agreement with stones. Laban's name for the heap is Jegar-sahadutha and Jacob's is Galeed, "heap of testimony" (31:47). The other name is Mizpah: "May the Lord keep watch on us when we are unable to see one another's doings" (31:49). The agreement is in the court's own words: "God will be the witness between us" (31:50), "be our judge" (31:53). Then Laban "went on his way back to his country" (31:55).

What it means: You do not simply walk away from the old state. You file a boundary with it, with the court as witness: neither crosses the stones for harm (31:52). And you will not see the old state again from where you now stand, which is why the watch is set "when we are unable to see". The old state goes back to its own ground, and Jacob goes on.

9. House-of-El: the gods put away, a new name (35:1-15)

The text: Jacob says to his people "Put away the strange gods which are among you" (35:2), and he puts them away under the tree at Shechem (35:4). At Beth-el (H1008, "house of God", from bayith and El) the name changes: "Jacob is your name, but it will be so no longer; from now your name will be Israel" (35:10). Then comes "be fertile, and have increase" (35:11), the words of the blessing in Genesis 1:22 and 1:28.

What it means: The old judges have to be given up before the new name is given, and the order is in the text: gods put away first, then the name. Jacob, the one who grabs a place, stops being what he is called. The new name, Israel, "he will prevail", is the first to be spoken the Genesis 1 blessing. The grabber never got it. The identity that is held, and not grabbed, gets the first blessing.

10. Rachel on the road (35:16-20)

The text: "While they were still some distance from Ephrath, the pains of birth came on Rachel" (35:16). Ephrath is "fruitfulness". She names the son Ben-oni, "son of my sorrow", and "his father gave him the name of Benjamin", "son of the right hand" (35:18). She is "put to rest on the road to Ephrath (which is Beth-lehem)", House-of-bread, with a pillar over her resting-place (35:19-20).

What it means: Her first words in chapter 30 were "If you do not give me children I will not go on living", and here the one who said it is in labour, a distance short of Fruitfulness. The Ewe, a good traveller, is the state that is always on the way, and it comes to rest on the way, before arriving. It cannot carry the arrival. Its last fruit is named by its own state: sorrow. The father, who now holds the new name, gives the fruit his own name instead: the right hand, the place of strength. What the picture bears is named by the one who is now standing in the new name, and the picture is put to rest at the place of fruitfulness and bread.

11. Where each is put to rest, and what the two are called (49:29-31, Ruth 4:11)

The text: Jacob asks to be put to rest in the hollow of the rock in Ephron's field, where "Abraham and Sarah his wife were put to rest, and there they put Isaac and Rebekah his wife, and there I put Leah to rest" (49:29-31). Ruth 4:11 puts the two together: "May the Lord make this woman... like Rachel and Leah, which two were the builders of the house of Israel: and may you have wealth in Ephrathah, and be great in Beth-lehem." "Builders" is the same banah as Rachel's "have a family" in 30:3. See the Ruth lesson.

What it means: The weary state ends in the family place with the fathers, and the traveller ends on the road. Yet neither is the loser. Both are named the builders, and the word is the one Rachel used when she wanted to be built through another. The house of the new name is built by the one he pictured and by the one he did not choose. The verse that names them puts Ephrathah and Beth-lehem beside them, the place where the traveller was put to rest.

Jacob's sons, in order of birth

Each name is followed by its meaning and what the mother says (BBE).

Leah's first four:

  • Reuben (29:32), "see ye a son": "The Lord has seen my sorrow."
  • Simeon (29:33), "hearing": "It has come to the Lord's ears that I am not loved."
  • Levi (29:34), "attached": "Now at last my husband will be united to me."
  • Judah (29:35), "celebrated (praise)": "This time I will give praise to the Lord."

Bilhah's two (Rachel's servant):

  • Dan (30:6), "judge": "God has been my judge."
  • Naphtali (30:8), "my wrestling": "I have had a great fight with my sister, and I have overcome her."

Zilpah's two (Leah's servant):

  • Gad (30:11), "troop": "It has gone well for me" (KJV: "A troop cometh").
  • Asher (30:13), "happy": "Happy am I!"

Leah's next two:

  • Issachar (30:18), "he will bring a reward": "God has made payment to me."
  • Zebulun (30:20), "habitation": "Now at last will I have my husband living with me."

Rachel's two:

  • Joseph (30:24), "let him add": "May the Lord give me another son."
  • Benjamin (35:18), "son of the right hand": she said Ben-oni, "son of my sorrow"; his father said Benjamin.

Read down Leah's first four: see, hear, attached, praise. Read down the servants' sons: judge, wrestling, troop, happy. Leah's four are her own asking. The servants' are the two sisters comparing.

What this means for you

You hold more than one identity at once, and they are not equal in your eyes. One looks right and you can picture it. The other is older, plainer and tired, and it is the one you are joined to first, in the dark, before you can see it. The story does not say the picture is wrong. It says the picture is not the first thing you are in.

The Existing One, you aware right now, sees the one you have written off as unloved. In the story it is Leah who bears first, and her four names run from being seen to being heard to being attached to praise. The loved one waits, and when you set one identity against the other (envy, wrestling, paying with love-fruits), the names that come out are contest names. The court gives back what is actually held.

When the leaving comes, the identities themselves say the father's house has no part for them, but one of them still carries its gods. Those are put away (35:2-4) before the new name is given (35:10). The traveller is put to rest on the road, a short way from Fruitfulness, and what she bore takes the name of the one who now holds the new name. Both sisters are then called the builders of the one house. The picture and the weary one are not rivals in the end. Both build it.

What changed from Genesis 1

Lesson 5 says every story is the Genesis 1 picture, changed. Here is each part, with Genesis first and this scene second.

  • The day. Genesis: "There was evening and there was morning" (1:31). Here: joined in the evening, seen in the morning (29:23, 29:25).
  • The seeing. Genesis: the Judges see what has been made, and it is good (1:31). Here: Jacob sees Rachel and loves her (29:17-18). The Existing One sees Leah, not loved, and opens her womb (29:31).
  • The woman. Genesis: the woman is built (banah, 2:22). Here: Rachel asks to be built through her servant (30:3).
  • The union. Genesis: "Be joined to his wife; and they will be one flesh" (2:24). Here: joined to the older first (29:23), then Levi, "attached" (29:34), then Zebulun, "living with me" (30:20).
  • The naming. Genesis: the man names the woman (2:23). Here: the women name the sons, and the father renames the last (29:32 to 30:24, 35:18).
  • The blessing. Genesis: "Be fertile and have increase" (1:22, 1:28). Here: spoken over Israel at Beth-el (35:11), and Rachel is in labour five verses later (35:16).

Three questions for this story

  1. What is the ground, and what does its name mean? White's country, the old family state, and then House-of-El and Fruitfulness at the end of the road.
  2. Who is standing on it, and what do their names mean? Heel-catcher with Weary and Ewe, two servants, and twelve sons whose names say what each identity brought forth.
  3. What has changed from the Genesis 1 picture? The man joins in the dark the one he did not choose, the one he pictured comes second, and the old gods are put away before the new name.

Remember: the identity you picture is not the first one you are joined to. The Existing One sees the weary one, the court gives back what is held, and in the end both build the house.

  6. Read Ruth 4:11 and Genesis 49:31 beside Genesis 35:19.
  7. Ask whether the reading fits the words on the page. If it does not, say so.

Common questions

Why did Laban give Jacob Leah instead of Rachel?

White (Laban) gives the reason in 29:26: "In our country we do not let the younger daughter be married before the older." Heel-catcher (Jacob) had worked seven years for Ewe (Rachel), and in the evening White gave him Weary (Leah). In the morning Heel-catcher saw who it was (29:23-25). In this course Weary is the older identity, joined first, in the dark, and Ewe, the one he can picture, comes second. What comes out of the night is the same kind as the night: he passes through the weary state he was already in before he reaches the picture.

How many years did Jacob work for Leah and Rachel?

Fourteen. Heel-catcher worked seven years for Ewe, then Weary was given (29:18-25). After the week of the bride-feast Ewe was given, and seven more years of work followed (29:27-30). He says it himself: "fourteen years because of your daughters" (31:41). The picture is not swapped in for the weary state. It is added after, with more work.

What was wrong with Leah's eyes in Genesis 29:17?

The BBE says "clouded". The Hebrew is rak (H7390), "tender; by implication, weak", which is why the KJV has "tender eyed". Ewe is "fair in face and form": mar'eh (H4758, "appearance") and to'ar (H8389). Mar'eh comes from ra'ah, "to see", the same root as the Existing One seeing that Weary was not loved (29:31). Heel-catcher sees by appearance. The Existing One, you aware right now, sees by the same root what has no appearance: the state you are actually in. Seeing it is what opens it.

What do the names of Jacob's sons mean?

Weary's first four run from seen to praised: See-a-son (Reuben), Hearing (Simeon), Attached (Levi) and Praise (Judah), 29:32-35. Timid's (Bilhah's) two are Judge (Dan) and My-wrestling (Naphtali), and Trickling's (Zilpah's) two are Troop (Gad) and Happy (Asher), 30:6-13. These are the two sisters comparing. Weary's next two are He-will-bring-a-reward (Issachar) and Habitation (Zebulun), 30:18-20. Ewe's two are Let-him-add (Joseph) and Son-of-the-right-hand (Benjamin), 30:24 and 35:18. Each mother says why she chose the name, and the list of sons above gives her words beside each one.

Why did Rachel take her father's idols in Genesis 31:19?

Ewe and Weary both say there is no part or heritage for them in their father's house (31:14), yet Ewe secretly takes the images of the gods of her father's house (31:19). In this course the gods are the Judges, the ones who make a claim real, so the identity that says leave is still carrying the old family state's judges, hidden under her. That is why White can chase and search the tents (31:30-35). Heel-catcher does not know she has them, and his own sentence of death falls on the one he loves (31:32). The new state is held anyway, because the Judges themselves tell White to say nothing to Heel-catcher (31:24). The gods are put away (35:2-4) before the new name, Israel, is given (35:10).

How did Rachel die, and what do Ben-oni and Benjamin mean?

Ewe died in labour on the road, some distance from Fruitfulness (Ephrath), which is House-of-bread (Beth-lehem), and was put to rest there (35:16-19). As her life went she named the child Son-of-my-sorrow (Ben-oni, H1126), and "his father gave him the name of Benjamin", Son-of-the-right-hand (H1144). Ewe, a good traveller, is the state that is always on the way, and it comes to rest on the way, before arriving. Her last fruit is named by her own state: sorrow. The father, who now holds the new name, gives it his own: the right hand, the place of strength.

Where were Leah and Rachel buried?

Ewe was put to rest on the road to Fruitfulness, with a pillar over her resting-place (35:19-20). Weary was put to rest in the hollow rock in the field of Ephron, with Abraham and Sarah and Isaac and Rebekah, and Heel-catcher asks to be put there too (49:29-31). Neither is the loser: Ruth 4:11 names both as the builders of the house of Israel.
