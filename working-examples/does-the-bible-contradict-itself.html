Does the Bible Contradict Itself? Three Famous Examples

The Bible has pairs of verses that look like they clash, and people search for them all the time. In this course a clashing pair is not two writers disagreeing. It is one story told to one reader, from two different places in the plot. Put the reader inside the scene and the pair stops clashing. Below are three famous ones, each read that way.

Three questions to ask of any pair:

  1. Whose side is each verse told from? The scene as it is laid out, or the one standing in it.
  2. Which name does each verse use? Sometimes the text names two different things and the English hides it.
  3. Is the mind changing, or holding? A held state stands. A changing mind moves.

1. Genesis 1 and 2: Who came first, the man or the plants and animals?

This pair is answered by question 1: whose side is it told from?

The passage (BBE, selected verses):

And grass came up on the earth, and every plant producing seed of its sort, and every tree producing fruit, in which is its seed, of its sort: and God saw that it was good. (1:12) And God made the beast of the earth after its sort, and the cattle after their sort, and everything moving on the face of the earth after its sort: and God saw that it was good. (1:25) And God said, Let us make man in our image, like us: and let him have rule over the fish of the sea and over the birds of the air and over the cattle and over all the earth and over every living thing which goes flat on the earth. (1:26) In the day when the Lord God made earth and heaven there were no plants of the field on the earth, and no grass had come up: for the Lord God had not sent rain on the earth and there was no man to do work on the land. (2:5) And the Lord God made man from the dust of the earth, breathing into him the breath of life: and man became a living soul. (2:7) And from the earth the Lord God made every beast of the field and every bird of the air, and took them to the man to see what names he would give them: and whatever name he gave to any living thing, that was its name. (2:19) — Genesis 1:12, 25-26; 2:5, 7, 19 (BBE)

The clash: in Genesis 1 the plants and the animals come first and the man comes last. In Genesis 2 there is no plant of the field yet because there is no man to work the land, then the man is made, and only after that are the animals brought to him.

How to read it, step by step:

  1. Genesis 1 is the scene. Lesson 5 (The Original Plot) explains that the whole setting is built and called good before the identity is placed in it. Genesis 1 tells you what is in place. It uses one name, "God", the judges.
  2. Genesis 2 is the same moment told from the one standing in it. It says "in the day when the Lord God made earth and heaven" (2:5), so it is the same day, not a later one. The name has changed to "the Lord God", the Existing One together with the judges (Lesson 2). Now the reader is in the picture. The man is put in the garden in Eden (2:8), and Eden means "pleasure" (H5731), the ground he stands on.
  3. The animals are met, not made twice. Genesis 1 lists them as part of the scene. Genesis 2 shows what happens next from the man's side: each thing in the scene is brought to him, and "whatever name he gave to any living thing, that was its name" (2:19, BBE). The order in Genesis 2 is the order of meeting: you are there first, then each part of the scene comes to you.
  4. The plants. Genesis 1 lists them as part of the scene. Genesis 2:5 says no plant of the field had come up because there was no rain and "no man to do work on the land". In this reading the scene is laid out, but it only becomes a field when someone is standing in it to work it.
  5. The same goes for "male and female". Genesis 1:27 says "male and female he made them", and Genesis 2:18 says "It is not good for the man to be by himself", with the woman made afterwards (2:22). The identity is made whole in chapter 1, and chapter 2 follows that same identity step by step, alone first and then joined (2:24, Lesson 8).

2. Pharaoh's heart: Did God harden it, or did Pharaoh?

This pair is answered by question 3: is the mind changing, or holding?

And I will make Pharaoh's heart hard, and my signs and wonders will be increased in the land of Egypt. (7:3) But when Pharaoh saw that there was peace for a time, he made his heart hard and did not give ear to them, as the Lord had said. (8:15) — Exodus 7:3; 8:15 (BBE)

The clash: the Lord makes the heart hard, and Pharaoh makes it hard himself.

How to read it: the heart is the mind (leb, H3820), and a hard heart is an unchanged one, which is what the Lord says in 7:14 ("Pharaoh's heart is unchanged", BBE). The Existing One (the Lord, H3068) is you, aware right now. So the Lord making it hard and Pharaoh making it hard are one event from two sides: you as you are now, and the mind that holds the state. The mind stays as it is, and the signs keep showing that state back to it. There is nothing to reconcile. Lesson 18 walks through the whole passage.

3. 1 Samuel 15: Does God change his mind or not?

This pair is answered by question 2: which name does each verse use?

It is no longer my pleasure for Saul to be king; for he is turned back from going in my ways, and has not done my orders. (15:11) And further, the Glory of Israel will not say what is false, and his purpose may not be changed: for he is not a man, whose purpose may be changed. (15:29) And Samuel never saw Saul again till the day of his death; but Samuel was sorrowing for Saul: and it was no longer the Lord's pleasure for Saul to be king over Israel. (15:35) — 1 Samuel 15:11, 29, 35 (BBE)

The clash: in 15:11 and 15:35 the Lord has changed about Saul, and in 15:29 the "purpose may not be changed". The BBE smooths this over. In the Hebrew it is one verb, naham (H5162, to sigh or be sorry), used in 15:11 ("it repents me"), twice in 15:29 ("will not lie nor repent... not a man that he should repent", KJV) and in 15:35. So in the Hebrew, one chapter says the Lord is sorry and says he is not.

How to read it, step by step:

  1. The text names two different things. In 15:11 and 15:35 it is "the Lord", the Existing One (H3068), you aware right now. In 15:29 Samuel names someone else: "the Glory of Israel", netsach (H5331), which Strong's gives as splendour, victory and enduring, "continually". One is the mind as it is now. The other is what endures.
  2. The present mind can be sorry. The reason given is that Saul "is turned back from going in my ways" (15:11). Saul means "asked" (H7586), the one asked for, and he turns back from what he was set up to do by keeping the best of what he was told to put away (15:9).
  3. What endures does not turn. Saul says "Great is my sin" and asks for forgiveness (15:24-25), but Samuel (heard of God, H8050) says the kingdom is taken (15:28), and that the purpose "may not be changed" (15:29). Saul's sorrow is only in words: a few verses later he says "give me honour now before the heads of my people" (15:30). The mind has not changed, and that is the same unchanged mind as Pharaoh's.
  4. Both are true in one chapter. The present mind can be sorry, and the standing ruling does not turn. They are not one thing said two ways. The text gives them two names.

These are three examples, not a proof of every pair. The way to test the reading is to take any pair that seems to clash and ask the three questions: whose side is it told from, which name is used, and is the mind changing or holding.

Remember: a pair of verses clashes when you read it as events outside you told by different writers. It stops clashing when you put yourself inside the scene: the scene and the one standing in it, the mind now and the mind that holds, the one who is sorry and the one who endures.

The meanings used: the Lord (YHVH) is "the existing One" (H3068). Heart is leb (H3820), the mind. Pharaoh is parʿô (H6547), "great house", a title. Eden is "pleasure" (H5731). Naham (H5162) is "to sigh, be sorry". The Glory of Israel is netsach (H5331). Saul (H7586) is "asked". Samuel (H8050) is "heard of God". Quotes are BBE, except where the KJV is named.
