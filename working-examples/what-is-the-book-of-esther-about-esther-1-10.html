What Is the Book of Esther About? Esther 1-10

People ask what the book of Esther is actually about, because it is a book where the name of the court never appears. The BBE text of the book does not print "God" or "Lord" once. What it shows instead is a court at work: a ruling seat, a law that cannot be changed, and a seal. Read the passage first, then read it again with the meanings put in.

The short answer: once a verdict is filed and sealed, it stands. Haman files one against a whole people, and the king's law says a sealed writing "may not be changed". So the story does not cancel the verdict. A second writing goes out under the same seal, and the first one turns back on the man who made it. Read from your side, Esther is the identity you have kept hidden, Haman is the state that has been lifted up and is filing the verdict, and the rest of the book is that identity stepping into the scene and a new filing taking over (About this reading).

The story in six steps (BBE):

  1. The king's queen, Vashti, will not come when sent for. A law is made that she may never come before him again (ch. 1).
  2. Esther is made queen. Her cousin Mordecai has told her to say nothing of her people (ch. 2).
  3. Haman is lifted up. Mordecai will not go down before him, so Haman gets a decree to destroy all the Jews on a date chosen by lot (ch. 3).
  4. Mordecai tells Esther she may have come to the kingdom for this time. She fasts three days and goes in to the king (ch. 4-5).
  5. Haman is made to honour Mordecai, then is hanged on the pillar he built for him (ch. 6-7).
  6. A second writing goes out under the king's ring. On the day of the decree, the opposite comes about, and the days are kept as Purim (ch. 8-9). Mordecai is second only to the king (ch. 10).

The passage (BBE, selected verses):

...let it be recorded among the laws of the Persians and the Medes, so that it may never be changed, that Vashti is never again to come before King Ahasuerus... Esther had not said what family or people she came from, for Mordecai had given her orders not to do so. ... Haman, the son of Hammedatha the Agagite, was lifted up and given a position of honour and a higher place than all the other captains who were with him. ... they went on looking for a sign given by Pur (that is chance) before Haman... who is to say that you have not come to the kingdom even for such a time as this? ... So Haman was put to death by hanging him on the pillar he had made for Mordecai. ... for a writing signed in the king's name and stamped with the king's ring may not be changed. ... And the Jews had light and joy and honour. ... the opposite had come about, and the Jews had rule over their haters... — Esther 1:19, 2:10, 3:1, 3:7, 4:14, 7:10, 8:8, 8:16, 9:1 (BBE)

The meanings used:

  • Esther (H635): "star". Strong's calls it "of Persian derivation", and the Persian word is stara, "star". Some scholars think instead of the Akkadian goddess Ishtar. The Bible's own word for star (kokab, H3556) is never used for her, so the star link below comes from the story, not from a shared word.
  • Hadassah (H1919): "myrtle". Brown-Driver-Briggs calls it the Jewish name of Esther. It occurs once, at 2:7: "Hadassah, that is Esther". It is the only Hebrew name in this group.
  • Vashti (H2060): Strong's says "beautiful" and "of Persian origin". Brown-Driver-Briggs links it to a Persian (Zend) word for "best", and later scholars read it as an Old Persian word for "best, excellent". Scholars call the meaning uncertain. The page uses "Best" because in most translations 1:19 hands her place to someone "better than she".
  • Haman (H2001): "magnificent". Strong's says "of foreign derivation". Scholars offer several origins (Persian "illustrious", an Elamite god, others) and none is settled. The page keeps "Magnificent" because he is "lifted up" (3:1).
  • Hammedatha (H4099): Blue Letter Bible lists "double", but Brown-Driver-Briggs reads the Persian parts as "moon" plus "given" (with a question mark), and the Bible encyclopedias say "given by the moon". The page uses "Given-by-the-moon". The entry includes the article ("the").
  • Agagite (H91): "I will overtop" (Blue Letter Bible). Strong's gives it as patrial or patronymic from H90 (Agag). The page reads Agagite as "of Agag" through the name alone.
  • Mordecai (H4782): Strong's gives only "of foreign derivation". Lexicons tie it to Marduka, a Persian-era name that carries the Babylonian god Marduk. "Little" does not come from the language. The page uses "Little Man" because of the story (see "Why Little Man?" below).
  • Jew (H3064): "a descendant of Judah". Strong's gives it as patronymic from H3063, and Judah (H3063) is "praised" (from H3034, "celebrated"). The page reads it as "Praised-line". In 2:5 Mordecai is called both "a Jew" and "a Benjamite", so in this book the word is an identity label.
  • Pur / Purim (H6332): "a lot (as by means of a broken piece)". The BBE glosses Pur as "chance" in 3:7 and 9:24.
  • Shushan (H7800): "lily", the palace-city where the book takes place.
  • Ahasuerus (H325): a Persian title, not a personal name, in Strong's. It is not replaced above.

A quick note on the names. Esther, Vashti, Haman, Mordecai and Hammedatha come from Persian (and in places Babylonian or Elamite), not Hebrew, so their meanings come from the lexicons and from those languages, and not from a Hebrew root. Where the origin is uncertain, the page picks the meaning the story itself backs up. Haman is "magnificent" because he was "lifted up" (3:1), and Mordecai is "little" for the reasons below, because the book says he "became greater and greater" (9:4).

How to read it, step by step

  1. The ground and the filing. The scene is Shushan, "lily", a palace of white and green and blue hangings (1:6). It is a finished scene before any identity is placed in it. Then Best is removed by a law "recorded" so that it "may never be changed" (1:19). From the first chapter the book is telling you how this court works: what is written and sealed stands. The same idea comes back in 8:8.
  2. The identity that stays hidden. Esther, "star", is put in the scene but "had still said nothing of her family or her people" (2:20). Her other name, Hadassah, "myrtle", is given once (2:7: "Hadassah, that is Esther") and the book then uses Star. She holds the place of queen without the identity that matters being on file with the king. Mordecai, the Little Man, sits in the doorway (2:19) and gives the orders (2:10, 2:20).
  3. The lifted-up one files the verdict. Haman, "magnificent", son of Given-by-the-moon, the Agagite, "I will overtop", is "lifted up" and given "a higher place than all the other captains" (3:1). All the king's servants "went down to the earth before Haman", but "Mordecai did not go down before him or give him honour" (3:2). Haman's reaction is out of all proportion. He makes it his purpose "to put an end to all the Jews" (3:6), the Praised-line. The state that overtops cannot stand one thing refusing to bow, so it files a verdict against all of it. The Overtopper then has a pillar "fifty cubits high" made for the Little Man (5:14). That is the same line as Lesson 17: a state that rules the scene turns on what will not go down.
  4. Chance sits where a claim should be. Haman looks "for a sign given by Pur (that is chance)" from day to day and month to month (3:7). Pur is a lot, "as by means of a broken piece". He does not hold a claim and wait. He lets the lot decide the day, and his father's name probably has the moon in it, the light that marks the months. On day four of Genesis the lights are the court's own signs for times (1:14, see below), so he is reading the times from the wrong place. And the date the lot picks, the thirteenth of Adar, is the same date the book ends the filing on: "the opposite had come about" (9:1). The lot did not decide anything. The court did.
  5. The identity steps into the scene. Mordecai says "who is to say that you have not come to the kingdom even for such a time as this?" (4:14). Esther goes in to the king, which "is against the law" (4:16), and the king holds out the gold rod (5:2). Then she says what she had kept back: "let my life be given to me in answer to my prayer, and my people at my request" (7:3). The Jews have been named in the book before this (3:4, 3:6, 3:13), and Haman's account to the king only said "a certain nation" (3:8). "My people" is the first time Esther puts her own identity on file with the king. In 8:1 she also "made clear what he was to her", so Mordecai comes before the king.
  6. The first writing turns back, and a second goes out. Haman is hanged "on the pillar he had made for Mordecai" (7:10), the pillar fifty cubits high of 5:14, and 9:25 gives this as the king's order that the design "was to be turned against himself". That is after its kind: what is made returns as the same kind. The seal changes hands too. In 3:10 the king gave his ring to Haman. In 8:2 he "took off his ring, which he had taken from Haman, and gave it to Mordecai". The law still cannot be changed, so the answer is a new writing in the same name (8:8). The Praised-line, marked for destruction in 3:13, then "had light and joy and honour" (8:16), the same honour Haman was given in 3:1. The month is "turned from sorrow to joy, and from weeping to a good day" (9:22).

The star in the rest of the Bible

Esther's name is the Persian word for star, so it is worth asking what a star is everywhere else. The Bible's own word for star (kokab) turns up 37 times, and the same picture keeps coming back:

  • The people. Abraham's seed is to be like the stars (Genesis 15:5, 22:17, 26:4; Exodus 32:13). Esther is the one carrying a hidden people.
  • The ones who bow. In Joseph's dream eleven stars bow to him (Genesis 37:9). In Esther, Mordecai is the one who will not bow (3:2).
  • The star out of Jacob. "a star will come out of Jacob, and a rod of authority out of Israel" (Numbers 24:17, BBE). The same song says "his king will be higher than Agag" (24:7, BBE) and "Jacob will have rule over his haters" (24:19, BBE). Esther ends with Mordecai set above the Agagite and the Jews ruling "over their haters" (9:1).
  • Lifted among the stars, then brought down. Isaiah 14:13 speaks of setting a throne above the stars, Obadiah 4 of a nest among the stars that is brought down, and Job 22:12 of the head of the stars, how high. That is the picture of Haman: lifted up (3:1), a pillar fifty cubits high (5:14), and hanged on it (7:10). The picture matches. I have not checked whether the Hebrew verbs match.
  • Turning many. Daniel 12:3 says those who turn many to righteousness will shine like the stars for ever. Esther's one act turns a verdict for a whole people.
  • A star that points out a king. The wise men say "We have seen his star in the east and have come to give him worship" (Matthew 2:2, BBE), and the star "came to rest over the place where the young child was" (2:9, BBE). Strangers name the Jewish king by his star. In Esther a foreign name carries a hidden Jewish identity in the same way.

From your side, the star is the identity that stays hidden in the night of the decree until it is declared.

Why "Little Man"?

The lexicons do not give "little" for Mordecai. The Bible keeps giving his line the same word, though:

  • Benjamin is the small one. Mordecai is a Benjamite (2:5), and 2:5 names Kish in his line, the same name as Saul's father (1 Samuel 14:51). The text does not say it is the same man. Saul says "Am I not a Benjamite, of the smallest of the tribes of Israel?" (1 Samuel 9:21, BBE), and later Samuel reminds him that he once saw himself as little (15:17).
  • Paul. Paul says he is "of the tribe of Benjamin" (Philippians 3:5, BBE), and Acts 13:9 says "Saul, who is also Paul". Paulos is a Latin name that Strong's glosses "little" (G3972). It is a Hebrew identity going by a foreign-language name, as Hadassah goes by Esther.
  • Agag. Saul spares Agag, the king of the Amalekites, in 1 Samuel 15. Samuel cuts him down (15:33) and Saul's kingship ends (15:35). In Esther, Mordecai, the little Benjamite, will not bow to the Agagite and ends "second only to the king" (ch. 10), which is the picture of "higher than Agag" in Numbers 24:7.
  • Little becomes great. Esther 9:4 says he "became greater and greater".

What changed from Genesis 1

Lesson 5 says every story is the Genesis 1 picture, changed. In Genesis 1 the scene is built and called good before the man is placed in it. The deep comes first:

And the earth was waste and without form; and it was dark on the face of the deep: and the Spirit of God was moving on the face of the waters. — Genesis 1:2 (BBE)

The star fits day four best. On that day the court sets lights in the sky "for signs, and for marking the changes of the year, and for days and for years" (Genesis 1:14, BBE), and in other translations the lesser light and the stars are set over the night (1:16-18). Esther the star and Mordecai the little one are in the night of the decree. Haman does the opposite: he goes looking for a sign by lot to pick his day, and the court's times are what stand.

What changed is that the scene is already made and ruled by a sealed law, and the identity placed in it is hidden. In Genesis 1 the man is placed and rules. In Esther the placed identity has to be declared before the scene turns, and the turn does not break the old law. It uses it, because a writing in the king's name can only be answered by another writing in the king's name.

Remember: the book never names the court, but it shows it. What is filed and sealed stands, so a verdict made by the lifted-up state has to be answered by a second filing under the same seal, and the hidden identity has to be put on record first. The first design returns to the one who made it, after its kind.

Words checked

  • The five Persian-origin names: meanings come from Strong's, Blue Letter Bible, Brown-Driver-Briggs, and Bible encyclopedias (the Iranica entry for Haman, the Jewish Encyclopedia and the International Standard Bible Encyclopedia for Hammedatha). The origins of Vashti, Haman and Mordecai are uncertain even among scholars. No inscriptions were read here.
  • Hammedatha: "double" (Blue Letter Bible) was replaced by "given by the moon", which Brown-Driver-Briggs gives with a question mark.
  • Star: all 37 places of kokab (H3556) were read from the concordance list, and the 24 places of the Greek word (G792). Esther's name (H635) is a different word, so the link is the story's picture, not a shared word.
  • Agag: H90 occurs 8 times in 6 verses (1 Samuel 15:8, 9, 20, 32, 33 and Numbers 24:7). Agagite (H91) is a separate entry. 1 Samuel 15 was read in the BBE only in part (15:32-35). The 15:17 wording is from other translations, and the BBE wording is not checked.
  • Paul and Saul: Acts 13:9 and Strong's G3972 ("little", of Latin origin) were checked. Philippians 3:5 and 1 Samuel 9:21 were read in the BBE.
  • Genesis 1:14 was read in the BBE. The wording of 1:16-18 about the lesser light and the stars is from other translations, and the BBE wording is not checked.
  • English matches only: "sign" in Esther 3:7 (BBE) and Genesis 1:14, and "rule over haters" in Numbers 24:19 and Esther 9:1, rest on the English. The Hebrew words have not been compared.
  • Pur: the BBE says "chance" in 3:7 and 9:24, and Strong's says "a lot". The page quotes the BBE and uses "lot" for the meaning.
  • Vashti, "better than she": this is from other translations of 1:19. The BBE wording is not checked.
  • Rule, light, good, and "may not be changed": these links to Genesis rest on the English picture. The Hebrew words have not been checked here.
  • Not yet checked: Jair, Shimei, Kish, Abihail, Hegai, Hathach, Zeresh, Memucan, Harbonah, Bigthan, Teresh, Benjamite, Nisan, Adar and Sivan. They are left unchanged in the passage.
  • The divine names: they do not appear in the BBE text of Esther, so the page does not read them in.
