What Did Jesus Mean by 'Foxes Have Holes'? Matthew 8:18-22

"The foxes have holes, and the birds of heaven have a resting-place; but the Son of man has nowhere to put his head" is Matthew 8:20, and the same saying is in Luke 9:58. People ask whether Jesus is saying he was homeless, or that following him means being poor. In this course the scene is read as taking place inside the reader. Existing-One-is-salvation is you aware right now, the voices that step forward are your own, and the saying is the answer to a promise one of them makes. The scene then runs the Genesis 1 order from the deep up to the man, and the same chapter names the man it has been describing. Read the passage first, then read it again with the meanings put in.

The passage (BBE, Matthew 8:18-22):

Now when Jesus saw a great mass of people about him, he gave an order to go to the other side. (8:18) And there came a scribe and said to him, Master, I will come after you wherever you go. (8:19) And Jesus said to him, The foxes have holes, and the birds of heaven have a resting-place; but the Son of man has nowhere to put his head. (8:20) And another of the disciples said to him, Lord, let me first go and give the last honours to my father. (8:21) But Jesus said to him, Come after me; and let the dead take care of their dead. (8:22) — Matthew 8:18-22 (BBE)

The same verses with the meanings put in:

Now when Existing-One-is-salvation saw a great mass of people about him, he gave an order to go to the other side. (8:18) And there came a writer and said to him, Master, I will follow you wherever you go. (8:19) And Existing-One-is-salvation said to him, The cunning ones have burrows, and the birds of heaven have an encamping-place; but the Son of the human being has nowhere to recline his head. (8:20) And another of the learners said to him, Lord, let me first go and give the last honours to my father. (8:21) But Existing-One-is-salvation said to him, Follow me; and let the dead take care of their dead. (8:22)

The meanings used: Jesus is Iesous (G2424), from the Hebrew Yehoshua (H3091), "Jehovah is salvation", which reads Existing-One-is-salvation (see Lesson 19). Scribe is grammateus (G1122), "a writer, i.e. (professionally) scribe or secretary". Thayer adds that in the Bible it means a man learned in the law and the sacred writings, an interpreter, a teacher. Follow, which the BBE gives as "come after", is akoloutheo (G190), "to follow one who precedes, join him as his attendant". Fox is alopex (G258), "a fox, i.e. (figuratively) a cunning person". Hole is pholeos (G5454), "a burrow or lurking-place". The birds' resting-place is kataskenosis (G2682), "an encamping, i.e. (figuratively) a perch", from kataskenoo (G2681), "to camp down". Man is anthropos (G444), "man-faced, i.e. a human being". Head is kephale (G2776), "the head (as the part most readily taken hold of)". Put, in "put his head", is klino (G2827), "to slant or slope, i.e. incline or recline". Disciple is mathetes (G3101), "a learner, i.e. pupil", from manthano (G3129), "to learn". See the Bible's court language.

Six notes on the meanings. First, the word counts. Fox (G258) is used three times in the New Testament: Matthew 8:20, Luke 9:58 and Luke 13:32. Thayer reads the first two as the animal and Luke 13:32 as a sly and crafty man, so the cunning sense is in the word's range, but this verse uses it for the animal. Hole (G5454) and resting-place (G2682) each occur twice, and both times it is in this one saying, Matthew 8:20 and Luke 9:58. Second, the KJV prints "nests" where the BBE prints "resting-place". The Greek is built from "to camp down" and Thayer's first meaning for it is "the pitching of tents, encamping", so the picture is a stopping place pitched for a while. Third, for the head, Thayer also lists a figurative sense, "supreme, chief", but in this verse the head is literal. This page does not rest on the figurative sense. For the verb, Thayer places Matthew 8:20 and Luke 9:58 under "to recline: the head, in a place for repose". Fourth, in Luke 9:57 the BBE calls the first speaker "a certain man", not a scribe. Matthew names him a scribe, so this page reads Matthew. Fifth, the passage names no town. The only place named is "the other side" (8:18). The country named when they arrive is "the country of the Gadarenes" (8:28, BBE). Strong's (G1046) says only that a Gadarene is an inhabitant of Gadara, and neither Strong's nor Thayer gives a meaning for the name Gadara. The manuscripts also differ here. Thayer notes that some read Gergesenes, which Strong's (G1086) ties to the Girgashites, and Thayer prefers Gadarenes in Matthew 8:28. This page does not claim a meaning for the place name. Sixth, the storm. The BBE says "storm" in 8:24, and the Greek word is seismos (G4578), "a commotion", which Thayer glosses "a shaking" and lists Matthew 8:24 as its use for a tempest at sea. Of its 14 uses, this is the one the KJV renders as a tempest. The other 13 are rendered earthquake.

Who is in the scene

The text names a great mass of people, a writer, another learner and the one they speak to. In this course's reading, where the many voices are gathered under one present awareness (see the court language), all of them are in you.

The text says only "a great mass of people". Reading them as voices in you is this course's reading, not something the verse states.

How to read it, step by step:

  1. The order. A great mass of people is around Existing-One-is-salvation, and he gives an order to go to the other side (8:18). The order is to cross. The text then says "his disciples went after him" (8:23, BBE) and does not say the crowd did. So the many voices gather round, and only some of them go.
  2. The writer's promise. The word for scribe (G1122) means "a writer", a man of words. He says: "I will come after you wherever you go" (8:19, BBE). The verb is akoloutheo (G190), and it is the same verb at 8:19, 8:22 and 8:23: he promises it, he is told it, and the learners do it. His promise has no condition in it, only "wherever". It is the voice in you that promises in words.
  3. He is answered with a list of places. Existing-One-is-salvation does not say yes or no, as in Matthew 22, where the question is also turned into a different one. He names who has what: "The foxes have holes, and the birds of heaven have a resting-place" (8:20, BBE).
  4. The foxes are day six. Genesis 1:25 (BBE): "the beast of the earth after its sort". The fox belongs to that class, and its place is a burrow, pholeos (G5454), "a burrow or lurking-place". The place goes with the kind. This is Rule One at work: each thing gives back what it is, and a state comes with its identity.
  5. The birds are day five. Genesis 1:20 (BBE): "let birds be in flight over the earth under the arch of heaven", and 1:21: "every sort of winged bird". The bird's place is an encamping-place, kataskenosis (G2682), a perch pitched for a while under that arch. So both creatures in the saying come from the Genesis 1 list, and each has a place that suits its kind.
  6. The human being has no kind-place. The word for man, anthropos (G444), is "man-faced, i.e. a human being", and Thayer uses it for a man as set apart from animals (Luke 5:10; Matthew 4:19; Matthew 12:12). Genesis 1 does the same. The creatures are made first, each after its sort, and then comes "let him have rule over the fish of the sea and over the birds of the air and over the cattle" (Genesis 1:26, BBE). The man is made last and set over them, not placed among them as one more kind. A kind has a den because a kind is fixed. The one who claims an identity has no den of his own to go back to, because his place is the identity he is holding. So "nowhere to put his head" means there is no old state to retreat to. The verb is klino (G2827), "incline or recline", and Thayer reads it here as laying the head down in a place of rest.
  7. The second voice puts the old state first. "Lord, let me first go and give the last honours to my father" (8:21, BBE). The father is the thing named in Genesis 2:24 (BBE): "For this cause will a man go away from his father and his mother and be joined to his wife; and they will be one flesh". The rule there is leave, then join. The answer is "Come after me; and let the dead take care of their dead" (8:22, BBE), and the verb is the same G190 as the writer's promise. In Matthew 4:22 the same verb is used with a father left behind. The old state looks after itself and does not need you in it. Leave first, then follow.
  8. Who goes. "His disciples went after him" (8:23, BBE), with the same verb again. The writer is not named again after 8:20, and the text never says whether he went. What it shows is the learners going, and what happens when they do.

What changed from Genesis 1

Lesson 5 says every story is the Genesis 1 picture, changed. Here the picture is run in order. The saying in 8:20 sets the man against the creatures, which is where Genesis 1 ends. The story then goes back down to the deep and builds up again.

Genesis 1This scene
The deep. "it was dark on the face of the deep" (1:2)"a great storm in the sea, so that the boat was covered with the waves" (8:24). The storm word, seismos (G4578), is "a commotion".
The word, then the result. "Let there be light: and there was light" (1:3)"he got up and gave orders to the winds and the sea; and there was a great calm" (8:26)
Water and land. "let the dry land be seen: and it was so" (1:9)"when he had come to the other side, to the country of the Gadarenes" (8:28)

The man, set over them. "let him have rule over the fish of the sea and over the birds of the air and over the cattle" (1:26)The Son of man has no den (8:20), is asleep in the storm (8:24), and the sea does his orders (8:27)

The first three rows are the pattern in Lesson 13: down into the sea, held in the boat, brought out on the other side. The man who has no den is asleep in the commotion (8:24). The learners, who have just left their places, say "destruction is near" (8:25, BBE), and he says "Why are you full of fear" (8:26, BBE). That is the rule Job states in Lesson 12: the fear that is held is the one that comes. Then the word is spoken and the calm follows, in the same shape as the first word in Genesis 1:3.

The fourth row is the foxes and birds again, from the other side. In Genesis 1 each creature is made after its sort, and in 8:20 each keeps the place that fits it. In 8:32 a land herd goes into the sea and ends there. A creature out of the place that fits its kind ends. The man has no kind-place, so the sea does not end him. It answers him.

The original scene against this one

Lesson 5 gives the original scene four parts: the ground, the seed, the stand and the time. In Genesis 1 they are all settled before the man appears, and each stage is called good. The same parts are in Matthew 8:18-34, but none of them has settled yet.

Genesis 1 is a scene of pleasure. The ground has its name, each creature is in its place, the man is put into a finished scene, and the court calls it good. Matthew 8 has all the same parts in the middle of a change. The ground is not yet named, the man has no place to stand, the sea is in commotion, a herd is out of its place, and the scene does not end on a verdict. It ends on a request to leave.

That fits Lesson 5: every later scene is the original picture, changed. In this course's reading it is what a change of state looks like from the inside. The old scene is coming apart before the new one has settled. What holds through it is the man's rule. The sea answers him (8:26) and he is named (8:29). What has not come back by 8:34 is the ground, the stand and the verdict. So the scene is a transition and not a return to the original.

The revelation is the outcome

The man is named three times in this chapter, and each name is more than the last.

  1. "The Son of man" (8:20, BBE). A human being, anthropos (G444), with no den.
  2. "What sort of man is this, that even the winds and the sea do his orders?" (8:27, BBE). It is asked by the men, and no one in the text has answered it yet.
  3. "you Son of God" (8:29, BBE). It is said by the two who had evil spirits, who were "so violent that no man was able to go that way" (8:28, BBE).

God in 8:29 is theos (G2316), which Strong's gives as "a deity" and also "figuratively, a magistrate", and it is the word the Greek Genesis uses for the Judges in Genesis 1:26 (Lesson 3). So the third name reads Son of the Judges. Genesis 1:26-27 (BBE) has "Let us make man in our image" and "in the image of God he made him". The one set over the creatures in Genesis 1 is the one made in the Judges' image. The chapter shows the man with no den, then lets the sea answer him, then has the voices that were blocking the road name him as what Genesis 1:27 made. The scene does not argue it. It runs the Genesis order, and the name comes out at the end.

Remember: the foxes and the birds each have a place that suits their kind, taken from the Genesis 1 list. The human being is made last and set over them, so there is no den to go back to. The voice that says "wherever" and the voice that says "first" are both told to leave and follow. Then the scene runs Genesis 1 in order, from the deep, to the word and the calm, to the land, to the creatures, to the man, and the man is named as the one made in the Judges' image. Set against the original scene of pleasure, it is still a change in progress: the ground, the stand and the verdict are not settled by the end.
