What Do "Son of God" and "Son of Man" Mean? Following the Two Words from Genesis

"Son of God" and "Son of man" are usually read as titles. This page asks a plainer question: where do the two ideas first sit side by side in the Bible, and what does the story do with them? The answer it finds is a three-step story. Genesis puts a "God side" and a "man side" together in one person. Genesis 6 shows what happens when the two sides meet badly, which Lesson 15 on the Nephilim reads in full. Then Luke and the Gospels put the two sides back together in one person.

The short answer: the Bible tells one story about a person who has two sides, a side made from the ground, the man who makes claims, and a side that belongs to the court, the judging side. The same two words, Elohim and adam, carry it all the way from Genesis 1 to Luke 3. "Son of man" is the side that stands on the ground and makes the claims. "Son of God" is the side the court gives and makes real. The story shows it by what each side does.

The passage with the meanings put in:

And God made man in his image, in the image of God he made him. (Genesis 1:27) Adam had been living for a hundred and thirty years when he had a son like himself, after his image. (Genesis 5:3) The sons of God (the sons of the Judges) saw that the daughters of men (the daughters of the man) were fair. (Genesis 6:2) The son of Seth, the son of Adam (the man), the son of God (the son of the Judges). (Luke 3:38) If you are the Son of God, give the word for these stones to become bread. (Matthew 4:3)

In the Hebrew, "God" in these verses is Elohim, which the course reads as "the Judges and Rulers", and "man" is adam, "the man", the one who makes claims. The BBE says "God" and "men" and hides that the same two words are doing the work.

One person with two sides (Genesis 1 to 3)

The first man is described two ways. He is made "in the image of God", in his image, and "in the image of God he made him" (1:27). He is also made of dust from the ground, which is why the Hebrew word for him and the word for the ground (adamah) sound alike. In 3:22 the court puts both in one sentence: "Now the man has become like one of us". The man is the ground side, and "us" is the court side. Right before that, in 3:21, the court clothes him in skin. The fig leaves and skins page follows that part.

How Genesis 1 and Genesis 2 describe the man

Genesis 1 says only "God" (Elohim) from the start to 2:3. Genesis 2:4 begins a new section, and from there the name is "the Lord God" (YHVH Elohim). The two descriptions of the man sit in those two sections.

  • Genesis 1:26-28 (Elohim). "Let us make man in our image" (asah, 1:26), and God created him (bara, three times in 1:27), male and female together. His job is to rule (radah) and subdue (kavash) (1:28). Dust and ground are not mentioned.
  • Genesis 2:5-25 (YHVH Elohim). The man is formed (yatsar) from dust (aphar) from the ground (ha-adamah) and becomes a living soul (nefesh chayyah) (2:7). His job is to work (avad) the garden and keep (shamar) it (2:15). The image is not mentioned.

Five things in the Hebrew stand out.

  • The two sections are joined. Genesis 2:4 uses both "created" (bara) and "made" (asah) in one verse, so the text itself ties the sections together.
  • The jobs use different words. Radah (H7287) is used 27 times in the Bible, and its first two uses are Genesis 1:26 and 1:28. Avad (H5647, "work, serve") first appears in Genesis 2:5, and its first five uses (2:5, 2:15, 3:23, 4:2, 4:12) are all work on the ground or the garden. Genesis 1 never uses it.
  • The ground side is shared with the animals. In 2:19 the beasts and birds are also formed (yatsar) from ha-adamah. The man becomes a nefesh chayyah in 2:7, and that is the same phrase Genesis 1:24 uses of the animals the earth brings forth, and 2:19 uses it of them again. The image is said only of the man (1:26-27).
  • The forming word is a potter's word. Yatsar (H3335) is used 63 times in the Bible. In Genesis it appears only in chapter 2, and only for the man (2:7, 2:8) and the beasts and birds (2:19). BDB describes the picture as a potter forming clay.
  • Eden is a place, not the material. In 2:8 the Lord God plants a garden "in Eden" and puts the man there, after he is formed. In 2:9 the trees grow out of the same ground. In 3:17 it is "the ground" (ha-adamah) that is cursed, and the word Eden is not in that verse. In 3:23 the man is sent out of Eden "to work the ground from which he was taken", the same two words as 2:5, avad and ha-adamah. In 3:19 he goes back to the ground, "for out of it you were taken", and the text calls him dust. Strong's gives Eden (H5731) as the same as the word for "delight". The BDB lexicon only says Hebrew "probably" linked the two, so "delight" is a likely reading and not a certain one.

A son after the image (Genesis 5)

Genesis 5 repeats the same shape one step down. God makes man "in the image of God" (5:1). Then Adam has a son "like himself, after his image" (5:3). So the word "son" in Genesis starts as likeness: a son is one who carries the image of the one who made him. Seen that way, Luke 3:38 calling Adam "the son of God" is not a new idea. Adam is the man who was made in God's image.

The two sides meet badly (Genesis 6)

Genesis 6 splits the two sides into two groups: "the sons of God" and "the daughters of men" (6:2). They come together, and the text says "when the sons of God had connection with the daughters of men, they gave birth to children" (6:4). Lesson 15 reads what happens there: the judging side chooses by looks, and what it breeds is a name. This page adds only one thing, about the words. In the Hebrew, the first group is the sons of the Elohim, the Judges, and the second is the daughters of ha-adam, the man. These are the same two words as Genesis 1 to 3. Verse 1 sets the scene with the same pair as well: ha-adam began to multiply on the face of the ground (ha-adamah). So Genesis 6 is not a new cast. It is the two sides of the first man, seen as two groups.

Luke closes the loop

Luke 3 does two things in a row. The voice from heaven says "You are my dearly loved Son" (3:22), and then the family line runs back, "the son of Enos, the son of Seth, the son of Adam, the son of God" (3:38). So the line ends where Genesis began: Adam, the man, called the son of God. Immediately after that, a voice tests the claim. "If you are the Son of God," the Evil One says, and asks for bread from stones (Matthew 4:3; Luke 4:3). In Genesis 3 a voice also offered an identity: "you will be as gods, having knowledge of good and evil" (3:5, BBE). The Hebrew word behind "gods" is the same Elohim, so the offer is to be as the Judges. In both, a voice talks about the God side of a person at the moment it is being tested.

The Son of man

In the Gospels Jesus speaks of himself as "the Son of man", and three of those verses are read for this page. He has "nowhere to put his head" while foxes and birds have homes (Matthew 8:20). He has "authority for the forgiveness of sins on earth" (Mark 2:10). He is "lord even of the Sabbath" (Mark 2:28). So the same person is the Son of God in the voice from heaven and the Son of man in his own mouth. Matthew 8:20 sets him against the animals the man named in Genesis 2:20, and Mark 2:28 names the seventh day of Genesis 2:2-3. That is the plot picking up the first man's story: the ground side, among the animals it named, is the one acting with the court's authority on earth and holding the court's seventh day.

What this means in the course's terms

In the course, a man claims an identity and the court makes it real (Lesson 6; Lesson 1). Read that way, "Son of man" is the side that stands in the story and claims, made of the ground. "Son of God" is the side the court gives and makes real. The Bible never lets either word stand alone for long. Genesis keeps them in one man, Genesis 6 shows them meeting badly as two groups, and the Gospels put them on one person again. That is why the two phrases travel together.

How the plot settles it

Put the story in order and the two titles explain themselves. In Genesis 1 the court makes the man in its image, and in Genesis 5:3 a "son" is one who carries the image of the one who made him. That is the Son of God side: the identity the court issues, which is why Luke 3:38 calls Adam "the son of God". In Genesis 2 the man is formed from the ground, works the ground and names the animals. That is the Son of man side: the one who stands in the story and makes the claims. The title is already in the Hebrew of Ezekiel 2:1 as ben-adam, "son of the man", the same adam.

Genesis 6 shows the two sides acting as two groups, and the judging side choosing by looks. The Gospels then put them back in one person, and each title appears where its side is acting. The voice from heaven and the test of the claim belong to Son of God (Luke 3:22; Matthew 4:3). The ground, the animals, the earth and the seventh day belong to Son of man (Matthew 8:20; Mark 2:10; Mark 2:28).

The order of Genesis 1 and Genesis 2 differs for the same reason. Genesis 1 is the court's order of making, with the man last and given rule. Genesis 2 follows the man on the ground, where he meets the trees, the animals and the woman in turn. Genesis 2:4 joins the two sections with both verbs in one verse, "created" and "made".

A question to ask of any story: which side of the person is speaking here, the ground side or the court side?

Remember: in Genesis the man is made in the image of God and from the ground. Adam's son is "after his image". In Genesis 6 the two sides are two groups, the sons of the Judges and the daughters of the man, and the judging side chooses by looks (Lesson 15). In Luke 3:38 Adam is "the son of God", and in the Gospels the same person is both Son of God and Son of man. The two titles are the two sides of the first man.

Limits: This page does not repeat Lesson 15's reading of Genesis 6, and nothing here is meant to replace it. "The Judges" for Elohim is the course's wording. The BBE hides two Hebrew verbs in Genesis 4:1 and 6:4 behind one phrase, "had connection with" (yada, "knew", and bo, "came in"), so the two verses do not share a word.
