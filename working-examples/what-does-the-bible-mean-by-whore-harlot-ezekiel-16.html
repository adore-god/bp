What Does the Bible Mean by "Whore"? The Woman From Genesis to Ezekiel 16

Short answer: the Bible has one woman, and the same question is asked of her every time: what is she joined to? The word English Bibles translate "whore", "harlot" or "loose woman" is zanah (H2181). It is used of a woman, but also of a people, a city, a land, and a person's own heart. In this course it names a divided joining, and the story that uses it never ends on the word: the same chapters call her wife, mother and "mine", and finish with her brought back to a first condition.

People search this as "what does whore mean in the Bible", "harlot in the Bible meaning", "your desire will be for your husband" and "Rahab the harlot". This is a thread, not one passage, so it is read in order: Genesis 1 to 5, the women of Genesis 25 to 38, Joshua and Ruth, then Ezekiel 16 and 23 and Hosea. The saying "as the mother is, so is her daughter" has its own worked example, so Ezekiel 16 is only followed here as far as the thread needs.

New here? The course in four lines.

  • Every story has three parts: someone present (the Existing One, which is you, aware right now), the identity they claim (I AM), and the judges who make it real (Elohim). Lesson 1 and Lesson 2.
  • Rule One, after its kind: what is planted gives back only what it is. Lesson 7.
  • Rule Two, leave and join: step away from the old state and join the new one (Genesis 2:24). Lesson 8.
  • Names and places are codes: the meaning tells you what the state is like before the story starts. Lesson 9.

Where this sits in the story

The thread starts before the garden is planted. Genesis 1:27 and 5:2 say male and female were made together and given one name, and Genesis 2:23 says the woman is "taken out of Man". It runs through the women who name their children, the women of Genesis 38 and Joshua 2, the prophets' sisters in Ezekiel 16 and 23, and Hosea. Because the woman is read as a state inside you, the same woman is met again and again with a different name and a different ground under her.

The story, step by step

1. One name for both (Genesis 1:27; 5:2)

And God made man in his image, in the image of God he made him: male and female he made them. — Genesis 1:27 (BBE)
Male and female he made them, naming them Man, and giving them his blessing on the day when they were made. — Genesis 5:2 (BBE)

Before the woman is taken out of the man in Genesis 2, male and female have been made together and given one name. So the woman is not a second person added to the story. She is the other half of what is named Man. Genesis 2:23 tells it from inside the garden:

And the man said, This is now bone of my bone and flesh of my flesh: let her name be Woman because she was taken out of Man. — Genesis 2:23 (BBE)

She is taken out of him, so she is what he can be joined to (Genesis 2:24).

2. The life-giver (Genesis 3:15, 3:20; 4:1)

And the man gave his wife the name of Eve because she was the mother of all who have life. — Genesis 3:20 (BBE)

The woman is the mother of everything that follows, and the mother gives back only what she is joined to, after its kind. That is why Genesis 3:15 speaks of "your seed and her seed" (BBE), in the verse that follows the serpent. The name Eve (H2332) is used only twice. The second time, in Genesis 4:1, she is conceiving Cain.

3. Desire and rule (Genesis 3:16; 4:7)

...still your desire will be for your husband, but he will be your master. — Genesis 3:16 (BBE)

The Hebrew for "desire" is teshuqah (H8669), from a root meaning to stretch out after. It is used three times in the whole Bible. The second is Genesis 4:7, in the next chapter, and it is said of sin:

If you do well, will you not have honour? and if you do wrong, sin is waiting at the door, desiring to have you, but do not let it be your master. — Genesis 4:7 (BBE)

The same pair sits in both verses: a desire stretching out, and something ruling. The same ruling verb, mashal (H4910), sits in both verses. The third use is Song of Solomon 7:10, which the lexicon (BDB) gives as the man's longing for the woman (see the Song of Solomon example). In this course the woman's desire is the identity reaching toward what it is joined to, ruled by whatever it stretches out after. That is why the question on this page never changes, and why the same words can be put to Cain about sin.

4. The women who name the children (Genesis 4, 29, 30)

In Genesis it is the woman who says the name, and usually gives the reason in the same breath, so the state is declared before the child has done anything. Genesis 30 is also where Jacob's sticks appear. Here are the namings this thread rests on:

Rebekah is not on this list, and the text is clear on why. In Genesis 25:25-26 the BBE says "they gave him the name of Esau" and "he was named Jacob", with no woman speaking. What Rebekah is given is the older and younger line: "the older will be the servant of the younger" (25:23). The woman with Tamar does give Perez his name (38:29), which is on the Judah and Tamar page. The mothers name these sons first, and Jacob speaks over each of them again in Genesis 49, which Lingua Divina covers in Jacob blesses his sons.

One word ties this table back to the beginning. Ha-pa'am (H6471), "this time", is what Adam says in Genesis 2:23: "This is now bone of my bone." Leah says it three times, each time about her husband: Levi (29:34), Judah (29:35) and Zebulun (30:20). BDB groups all four verses together as "now at length". The man's first words at the woman's coming and the woman's words about being joined open with the same word. Rachel's Ben-oni is the one naming her husband overrules.

5. The word itself (zanah, H2181)

Strong's gives zanah as "to commit adultery (usually of the female...); figuratively, to commit idolatry", with the root idea "highly fed and therefore wanton". Blue Letter Bible lists 93 uses in 81 verses. Reading the whole list changes the picture:

  • Women: Dinah, whose brothers say it of her (Genesis 34:31, the first use in the Bible), Tamar (Genesis 38:15, 24), Rahab (Joshua 2:1; 6:17, 25), Jephthah's mother (Judges 11:1).
  • A people: Israel at Shittim (Numbers 25:1), and "all Israel" at Gideon's ephod (Judges 8:27).
  • A city: Isaiah 1:21.
  • A land: Leviticus 19:29, and Hosea 1:2.
  • A person's own heart and eyes: the lexicon (BDB) lists Numbers 15:39 here.

So the word is not a label that sticks to women. It describes what any identity is doing when it is joined to more than one thing at once.

6. The mother, the daughter and the sisters (Ezekiel 16)

The full chapter is worked through on the mother and daughter page: the Hittite mother and Amorite father, the saying in 16:44, and the second record, the agreement of 16:8 and 16:60. This thread needs three things from it. First, the mother is the state, and the daughter is the next state, after its kind. Second, the sisters are in a fixed order, "Your older sister is Samaria... and your younger sister... is Sodom" (16:46, BBE). Third, the chapter that uses the word most ends by turning back:

And your sisters, Sodom and her daughters, will go back to their first condition, and Samaria and her daughters will go back to their first condition, and you and your daughters will go back to your first condition. — Ezekiel 16:55 (BBE)

"First condition" is qadmah (H6927), and it is never used in Genesis, so the link to the opening picture is a matching picture and not a word link. Sodom's own entry in 16:49 is also on the Sodom page.

7. Two sisters again: Oholah and Oholibah (Ezekiel 23)

Their names were Oholah, the older, and Oholibah, her sister: and they became mine, and gave birth to sons and daughters. As for their names, Samaria is Oholah, and Jerusalem, Oholibah. — Ezekiel 23:4 (BBE)

The chapter opens with "two women, daughters of one mother" (23:2) and then tells you to read the names ("As for their names"). Oholah (H170) is "her tent" in Strong's, an idolatrous sanctuary, and Oholibah (H172) is "my tent is in her", both from the word for tent (H168). The older is Samaria, the same Watch-station as in chapter 16, and the younger is Jerusalem. Then:

And her sister Oholibah saw this, but her desire was even more unmeasured, and her loose behaviour was worse than that of her sister. — Ezekiel 23:11 (BBE)

The younger sees the older state played out and does it worse. Zanah is used of Oholah in 23:5, while she was still the Existing One's own (the same claim as "they became mine" in 23:4 and "you became mine" in 16:8), and again in 23:44. The pairing is not new. It is Leah the older and Rachel the younger ("the name of the older was Leah, and the name of the younger was Rachel", Genesis 29:16, BBE), Rebekah's two in one body (25:23), Samaria and Sodom (Ezekiel 16:46), and now Oholah and Oholibah. In each pair there is a first state and the one that follows it, and the text keeps asking which one the woman is joined to.

8. Hosea: the same woman, bought back (Hosea 1-3)

And the Lord said to Hosea, Go, take for yourself a wife of loose ways, and children of the same, for the land has been untrue to the Lord. — Hosea 1:2 (BBE)
So he took as his wife Gomer, the daughter of Diblaim, and she gave birth to a son. — Hosea 1:3 (BBE)

With the meanings put in, Deliverer (H1954) takes Completion (H1586). Notice who the lovers are in Hosea 2:5 (BBE): "For their mother has been untrue... she said, I will go after my lovers, who give me my bread and my water, my wool and my linen, my oil and my wine." The lovers are the givers of food and clothing, the two things Genesis 3 ends with (the food from the ground in 3:17-19, the coats of skins in 3:21; see fig leaves and skins). The woman goes to the wrong source for what she was already given.

And the Lord said to me, Give your love again to a woman who has a lover and is false to her husband, even as the Lord has love for the children of Israel, though they are turned to other gods and are lovers of grape-cakes. — Hosea 3:1 (BBE)

"So I got her for myself for fifteen shekels of silver and a homer and a half of barley" (3:2, BBE). The one who is told to take the woman is the same one told to love her again and buy her back. The word and the return are in the same book.

9. Rahab and Tamar: the woman who ends up in the line

The same word, zanah, is used of both. Judah takes Tamar for a harlot because her face is covered (Genesis 38:15), and later he says:

Then Judah said openly that they were his, and said, She is more upright than I am, for I did not give her to Shelah my son. — Genesis 38:26 (BBE)

Rahab (H7343, "proud") is "a loose woman of the town" (Joshua 2:1, BBE), and the story ends this way: "so she got a living-place among the children of Israel to this day" (Joshua 6:25, BBE). Then the genealogy:

And the son of Salmon by Rahab was Boaz; and the son of Boaz by Ruth was Obed; and the son of Obed was Jesse; — Matthew 1:5 (BBE)

Both women are named by the word and both end in the line that carries on. See also whose wife will she be. On the Judah and Tamar page, the word for "upright" in 38:26 is shown to be used first of her. In this course that is a state re-filed: the same woman, joined to a new claim, is upheld and the seed goes on after its kind.

10. Stretched out, then covered: Ruth

Matthew 1:5 names Ruth in the same line as Rahab. In Ruth 3 the BBE says:

Now in the middle of the night, the man awaking from his sleep in fear, and lifting himself up, saw a woman stretched at his feet. — Ruth 3:8 (BBE)
And she answering said, I am your servant Ruth: take your servant as wife, for you are a near relation. — Ruth 3:9 (BBE)

The BBE gives the meaning, "take your servant as wife". The interlinear gives the act itself: "spread therefore your skirt over your handmaid", which is the Hebrew paras (H6566) with kanaph (H3671), skirt or wing. The lexicon (BDB) lists one other use of that act over a person, Ezekiel 16:8, where it is said of the Existing One: "I put my skirts over you... and you became mine" (BBE). Ezekiel 16 has the same order as Ruth 3. First the one lying stretched out:

And when I went past you and saw you stretched out in your blood, I said to you, Though you are stretched out in your blood, have life; — Ezekiel 16:6 (BBE)

Then the covering (16:8). In the prophets' stories of a woman's son it is the man who stretches out: "he got up on the bed, stretching himself out on the child, and put his mouth on the child's mouth... and the child's body became warm" (2 Kings 4:34, BBE), and the same act is in 1 Kings 17:21. The Hebrew shared by Ruth 3:9 and Ezekiel 16:8 is the spreading of the skirt. For Ruth's own name and story, see the Ruth example.

What this means for you

The woman is not someone else. She is the half of you that takes on what you are joined to, and whatever she is joined to is what she gives back. When that joining is divided, the text has a word for it, and the word is used of cities, lands and hearts as well as women.

But the word is never the end of the record. The same woman is a wife, a mother, a bride covered with a skirt, and a daughter bought back. The sisters go back to a first condition, and the line goes on through Tamar, Rahab and Ruth. A divided joining is not a fixed nature. The way back is always to be joined again, which is the half of Genesis 2:24 that says be joined.

Three questions for this story

  1. What is the ground, and what does its name mean? Watch-station and Burning, the older and younger sister states, with the garden's Man as the ground under both halves.
  2. Who is the woman joined to, and what do those names mean? Life-giver, Completion, Proud and Palm tree, each named for the state she is in.
  3. What has changed from the Genesis 1 picture? The joining is divided, and then it is covered, bought back and brought back to a first condition.

Remember: the woman is the identity you are joined to. The word names a joining that is divided, and the story ends with her brought back to the first condition.

Check this yourself

  1. Look up H2181 and read every verse on the list. Note who the word is used of.
  2. Read Genesis 3:16 and Genesis 4:7 side by side and see the desire and the ruling in both.
  3. Read Genesis 29:31 to 30:24 and note what each woman says when she names a child.
  4. Read Ezekiel 23:1-4 and 23:11, then Ezekiel 16:44-46 beside them.
  5. Read Hosea 1-3 in one sitting and see who is told to love her again.
  6. Read Ruth 3:8-9 and Ezekiel 16:6-8 side by side.
  7. Read Genesis 2:23 beside Genesis 29:34-35 and 30:20 and see the same opening word.
  8. Ask whether the reading fits the words on the page. If it does not, say so.

Common questions

What does whore mean in the Bible?

The Hebrew verb is zanah (H2181), 'to commit adultery... figuratively, to commit idolatry' in Strong's. It is used of women, but also of Israel as a people, of a city, of a land, and of a person's own heart and eyes. In this course it names a divided joining, not a fixed nature.

What does 'your desire will be for your husband' mean in Genesis 3:16?

The word is teshuqah (H8669), 'a longing', used only three times: Genesis 3:16, Genesis 4:7 and Song of Solomon 7:10. In this course it is the identity reaching toward what it is joined to.

Which women name their children in Genesis?

Eve names Cain and Seth, Leah names Reuben, Simeon, Levi, Judah, Gad, Asher, Issachar and Zebulun, and Rachel names Dan, Naphtali, Joseph and Ben-oni, whom Jacob renames Benjamin. Most give their reason in the verse. Rebekah does not name her sons in the text.

Who are Oholah and Oholibah?

Two sisters, 'daughters of one mother' (Ezekiel 23:2). The chapter says 'Samaria is Oholah, and Jerusalem, Oholibah' (23:4, BBE).

What was Hosea told to do?

To take 'a wife of loose ways' (Hosea 1:2, BBE), and later to 'give your love again' to a woman who is false to her husband (3:1), buying her for fifteen shekels of silver and barley (3:2).

Who were Rahab and Tamar?

Both are named with the word zanah in the text, Tamar in Genesis 38 and Rahab in Joshua 2. Judah says of Tamar, 'She is more upright than I am' (38:26, BBE), and Rahab is named in Matthew 1:5.
