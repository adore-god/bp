Are Online Bible Lexicons Accurate? Where Strong's, BDB and Thayer's Come From

Click a Hebrew or Greek word on most free Bible websites and you are shown the same handful of old books: Strong's Concordance (1890), the Brown–Driver–Briggs Hebrew lexicon (1906) and Thayer's Greek lexicon (1889). So the real question is not which website is right. It is what each book is, who edited the wording on the screen, and what the English Bible does with the word.

The short answer: the old dictionary entries are real, and you can check them. The parts to watch are the short summaries, devotional notes and edited wording some sites add around them, and the English word "God", which stands where the Hebrew says Elohim. This course reads Elohim as the judges and rulers (Lesson 3), so this page shows where that meaning sits in the sources and how you can check it.

Every story in the course runs on three parts: YHVH, the existing One, you aware right now; I AM, the identity you claim; and Elohim, the judges and rulers who make it real (Lesson 2). Names and places are read by their meaning (Lesson 9). Both rest on word entries, so the entry has to be the right one.

Elohim in short: what the sources show

Ask most search tools or AI what Elohim means and the usual answer is "God", "gods", or "a plural of majesty". Each of these points can be checked in the sources, and the course's reading starts from them:

  • The word is plural. Strong's calls Elohim (H430) the plural of H433, which it gives as "probably prolonged" from El (H410). The ending -im is the plural ending.
  • BDB's first sense is "rulers, judges". Its sense 1 is "plural in number": 1a rulers, judges; 1b divine ones; 1c angels; 1d gods. The "God" meaning is filed later, under "plural intensive" and senses 3 and 4.
  • "Supreme God" is a usage label. It is Strong's own wording and the entry gives no reason for it.
  • The verb is not always singular. It is singular in Genesis 1:1 and plural in Genesis 20:13 and 35:7. The English reads "God" in all three, where the Hebrew is Elohim, the Judges.
  • The English hides the word. The King James Bible has "God" 2,346 times and "judge" 5 times for Elohim.
  • The same root sits inside Bible names. Daniel, Elijah and Joel are built from El and Yah, and each name was carried by more than one person.

The sections below show each point with its source, and separate what the entries say from what websites and translators have added.

The old books are out of copyright, and the sites that publish them say the files are public domain. That is why nearly every free site shows Strong's, BDB and Thayer's, and why you rarely see the modern lexicons online.

Is Strong's Concordance accurate?

As an index, yes. It lists every place a word is used and gives each Hebrew or Greek word a number, so you can see every verse for yourself. As a dictionary it is short on purpose. Strong called his dictionaries "brief and simple" in his own preface, and many of its meanings are the English words the King James translators chose, not a full definition. So the course uses Strong's to find the word and its number, then reads the fuller entry.

The Elohim entry (H430) shows how this works. Strong's calls it the plural of H433 and opens with "gods in the ordinary sense". It then says the word is "specifically" used of "the supreme God", gives magistrates third and a superlative last, and the English renderings it lists include "judges". The words "supreme God" are Strong's own, so they are not something a website added. They describe a way the word is used, and the entry gives no reason for them. The root is El (H410). Strong's gives it as "strength" and "mighty", and BDB opens with "god, god-like one, mighty one". Nothing in the form of the word says "supreme". That sense reflects how the word is used and translated, not how it is built (Lesson 3).

Is BDB accurate?

BDB is a serious scholarly lexicon and the main source of the Hebrew meanings on these sites. Two things to know. It is over a hundred years old, and newer lexicons such as HALOT came after later advances in the study of Hebrew's related languages. And its entries carry the editors' own notes: which source a verse is from, and how other readers take a verse. Those notes are about verses, not about the word. Read the numbered senses in order first, and the notes second.

BDB's Elohim entry is sorted by grammatical number. Sense 1, "plural in number", has four parts: 1a rulers, judges; 1b divine ones; 1c angels; 1d gods. The "God" meaning is filed separately under sense 2, "plural intensive", and then under senses 3 and 4 as "the (true) God" and "God". "Plural intensive" is the editors' label for a plural form with a singular meaning. It is an explanation of the form, and the entry itself marks several verses as disputed (Exodus 22:27, Psalm 8:6 and Genesis 1:27 among them). Sense 1a cites Exodus 21:6, Judges 5:8 and Psalm 82:1 and 82:6.

Is Thayer's Greek lexicon accurate?

Thayer's is a careful nineteenth-century lexicon. Blue Letter Bible says on its own help page that the Online Bible, which supplied its data, changed Thayer's definitions about the Holy Spirit and the divinity of Christ because Thayer was a Unitarian. So the Thayer's text on that site is Thayer's with edits. The same page says Blue Letter Bible has added page images of the original Thayer's and Gesenius's lexicons, so you can read the originals.

What does each Bible website add?

  • Blue Letter Bible. Its Strong's data comes from Larry Pierce's Online Bible, which says it made about 15,000 corrections to Strong's. The "Outline of Biblical Usage" on each word page is Pierce's, built on the older lexicons. For Elohim it follows BDB's senses as a flat list and leaves out BDB's notes on which verses are disputed. The site is a ministry and states its faith position openly.
  • Bible Hub. It shows Strong's, the NAS Exhaustive Concordance and BDB as written. It also adds a short summary line at the top of each word page and a "Topical Lexicon", and the page does not say who wrote either. On the Elohim page the summary reads "God, gods, divine beings, judges", a list that is not in Strong's. The Topical Lexicon reads as devotional teaching, for example saying the plural fits the later teaching of a triune God, and saying the word "most frequently" takes singular verbs when it speaks of the one true God. Neither claim is in Strong's, the NAS block or BDB on the same page.
  • Bible Study Tools. Its page says the Hebrew lexicon is the Brown, Driver, Briggs, Gesenius Lexicon, though the menu calls it the NAS lexicon. The Greek lexicon is based on Thayer's and Smith's Bible Dictionary "plus others". It also hosts the BBE.
  • Bible Gateway. It carries translations licensed from their publishers, and only those it has permission to show. It says its choices are guided by a Christian statement of faith. The Strong's-numbered tool its newsroom names is the Mounce Reverse-Interlinear New Testament. It is mainly a place to read translations.

So the check is the same on every site: go to the dictionary line, not the summary or commentary around it.

What does the English do to the word? The test case, Elohim

English Bibles carry Elohim across as "God". The King James Bible translates Elohim (H430) as "God" 2,346 times and "judge" 5 times. The Greek word theos (G2316) is "God" 1,320 times out of 1,343. The BBE does the same:

At the first God made the heaven and the earth. — Genesis 1:1 (BBE)

With the word put in, where the BBE says "God" the Hebrew is Elohim, the Judges and rulers:

At the first the Judges (Elohim) made the heaven and the earth. — Genesis 1:1 (BBE, with Elohim put in)

The BBE is used in this course because it is plain English. Its word list is about a thousand words, which cannot always match a Hebrew word one for one, so the course checks the Hebrew behind the verse. Here is what each source shows for the word:

What this shows. BDB's first sense is "rulers, judges", and it is plural, as the word is. Strong's opens with "gods", and the English says "God". Three sources open three ways, so the first line a website shows is not the whole entry. BDB's later senses come with the editors' notes on how other readers take each verse (Genesis 1:27, for example, is marked with an "if"). The course starts from the plural word and its first sense, and checks it against the plot (Lesson 3).

Does Elohim always take a singular verb?

The usual reason given for reading the plural word as one "God" is that the verb is singular. That is what the Bible Hub Topical Lexicon says, and it is the idea behind the label "plural intensive". You can test it in an interlinear, which gives the person and number of each verb:

VerseElohim (H430)The verb
Genesis 1:1Masculine pluralbara (created, H1254): third person masculine singular
Genesis 20:13Masculine pluralhit'u (caused to wander, H8582): third person common plural
Genesis 35:7ha-elohim, masculine pluralnigl'u (appeared, H1540): third person common plural

So the singular verb is the usual pattern, and it is not the whole picture. In the two plural cases the King James Bible reads "God caused me to wander" and "God appeared". With the word put in, these are "the Judges (Elohim) caused me to wander" and "the Judges (Elohim) appeared", and the plural verb now matches the plural Judges. In the English "God" it does not show up.

The same word is also read as judges and gods in the verses BDB cites under sense 1a. The BBE has the word as "the gods of the house" [the Judges of the house] in Exodus 21:6, and in Psalm 82 it reads "he is judging among the gods" [he is judging among the Judges] (82:1) and "You are gods" [You are Judges] (82:6). In Psalm 82:1 the Hebrew has Elohim (H430) twice, once where English says "God" and once where it says "the gods", so both are the same word. Psalm 82:6 is the same word again (H430). The Bible Hub interlinear for Psalm 82:1 gives it as "God" and "the rulers" in the same verse. Jesus points to Psalm 82 in John 10:34-35, and Thayer's sense 4 for theos cites that passage for magistrates and judges. The BBE page does not show the Hebrew, so confirm Exodus 21:6 in an interlinear as well.

One and many in the same passage

The same switch between singular and plural happens inside Genesis 1:26-27. In the BBE it runs: "Let us make man in our image, like us" (1:26), then "God [the Judges] made man in his image, in the image of God [the Judges] he made him: male and female he made them" (1:27). The maker is "us" and "our", then "he" and "his". The man made in that image is "him", then "them".

With the word put in, Genesis 1:27 reads: "The Judges (Elohim) made man in his image, in the image of the Judges (Elohim) he made him: male and female he made them." The "us" and "our" of 1:26 are the Judges speaking together, and the "he" and "his" of 1:27 are the same Judges acting as one.

What the text shows: the form moves between singular and plural, sometimes in one verse, and the Hebrew gives no reason. What the course reads from it: one court made of many voices, acting as one (Lesson 3). That reading accounts for the singular verb in Genesis 1:1 and the plural verbs in 20:13 and 35:7 without treating either as an exception. The common explanation, that the plural expresses majesty (BDB's label is "plural intensive"), has to treat the plural verbs as exceptions. It is also an explanation of the form, brought to the word, and not something the entry shows. Both are readings, and the checks above are what you can test them against.

The same root inside the names

The root El (H410) is also inside Bible names. Strong's makes H433 "probably prolonged" from H410 and H430 the plural of H433, so these names carry the root of the word. Each entry gives its own parts:

So the El root of Elohim, the Judges, is built into all three names, with the Existing One (YHVH) in two of them (Elijah and Joel) and "judge" (Dan) in the third (Daniel). The same parts are carried by many different people in the story. A single outside being would not explain that. A set of identities and voices, each one carrying the same parts, fits it. The course reads names as compressed identity codes (Lesson 9) and the full case for Elohim as the judges and rulers is in Lesson 3. The entries above are the checkable part, and the reading of them is the course's.

Common questions about Elohim

Why is Elohim plural?

The word is plural in form: Strong's calls it the plural of H433, and the -im ending is the plural ending. BDB's first sense is also plural, "rulers, judges". The verb with it is singular in Genesis 1:1 and plural in Genesis 20:13 and 35:7.

Does Elohim mean "gods"?

"Gods" is one sense. Strong's lists "gods in the ordinary sense" first, and BDB has it as sense 1d, after "rulers, judges", "divine ones" and "angels". BDB lists Exodus 21:6 and Psalm 82:1 and 82:6 under rulers, judges, and the BBE has "gods" [the Judges] in Exodus 21:6, in the second half of Psalm 82:1, and in Psalm 82:6.

Is it a plural of majesty?

That is a common explanation, and BDB's version of it is the label "plural intensive". It explains the plural form with a singular meaning, but it is not a definition in the entry, and it sits awkwardly with the plural verbs in Genesis 20:13 and 35:7.

Where does "supreme God" come from?

It is Strong's own wording for how the word is "specifically" used. The entry gives no reason, and its root, El (H410), is given as "strength" and "mighty".

How can I check this myself?

Follow the steps in the next section, including the interlinear step for the verb. Each fact on this page can be found in the Strong's, BDB and Thayer's entries and the interlinear pages listed under Words and sources.

How to check any word yourself

  1. Find the Strong's number for the word in the verse.
  2. Read the Strong's line, then the BDB or Thayer's entry. Read the numbered senses in order. Blue Letter Bible shows page images of the original books.
  3. Count how the King James Bible translated it. Blue Letter Bible lists the counts, so you can see which English words hide which senses.
  4. Read the verse in the BBE with the chapter around it.
  5. Check the verb in an interlinear. It shows whether a plural word has a singular or plural verb, so you can see a claim about "usually singular" for yourself.
  6. See whether the story follows the meaning. A meaning that is right shows up in how the passage turns out.

What it means for the reader

The label on the page is not the word. Where your Bible says "God", the Hebrew is Elohim, a plural word whose first sense is rulers and judges. In this course that is the many voices inside you, acting as one court. Checking the entry yourself is how you see it, and it takes a few minutes.
