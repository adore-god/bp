What Happened on the Road to Emmaus? Luke 24:13-35

The point: two walkers leave Jerusalem on the first day, going over everything that has happened, and the one they are talking about walks beside them. Their eyes are not open to know him. He goes back through the Writings with them from Moses, they ask him to stay as evening comes, and at the table he takes bread, blesses it, breaks it and gives it. Then their eyes are opened and they know him. It is the Genesis 3 scene told in the same Greek words, with one change: in Genesis the eyes open on lack, and here they open on the identity that was beside them all along.

In five lines:

  1. Two of them go out of Jerusalem on the first day to a town named Emmaus, "warm baths" (24:13), going over what has happened (24:14).
  2. Jesus walks beside them and their eyes are "not open" to know him (24:15-16). All their talk is lack: "we were hoping" (24:21), "him they did not see" (24:24).
  3. He opens the Writings "from Moses" (24:27) and their hearts burn on the way (24:32). The Writings open before the eyes do.
  4. At the table he takes the bread, blesses it, breaks it and gives it (24:30). Then "their eyes were open" and they know him (24:31). It is the same Greek verb, in the same form, as Genesis 3:7.
  5. He goes from their view, and that very hour they go back to Jerusalem (24:33), keeping the knowing after the sight has gone. There they are told "Simon has seen him" (24:34).

The eyes are shut first, and the talk is all lack

The BBE says "their eyes were not open that they might have knowledge of him" (24:16). When the stranger asks what they are talking about, they answer with a list: a prophet, given up to the rulers, "we were hoping", three days gone, an empty tomb, "him they did not see" (24:19-24). It is the position of Genesis 3:10, where the answer to the question is "I was full of fear, because I was without clothing". In both scenes the one who comes asks first, and the answer is a statement of lack.

The father's name speaks, then drops out

The one who answers the stranger is named Cleopas, "of a renowned father" (24:18). Genesis 2:24 is the rule that a man goes away from his father and is joined to his wife, the identity he takes on (Lesson 8). A name built on a father's fame is the state that rule says to leave, and it is the voice doing the talking, in lack: "we were hoping" (24:21). Follow the name through the story. It is used once, here, and the walkers are never called by it again. After the bread they are only "they" (24:32-35). The father's name speaks while the eyes are shut and is not heard once they are open.

The Writings open before the eyes do

He goes through the Writings "from Moses and from all the prophets" (24:27). Luke does not record what he said, so this page does not claim to know. What the text does show is the order. The Writings are opened on the road, the hearts burn on the road (24:32), and only at the table do the eyes open (24:31). Genesis 3 runs the other way: the eyes open first and what they see is lack. One Greek verb covers the eyes (24:31), the Writings (24:32) and the minds (24:45), so the chapter is one opening in three places.

Bread with a blessing

At the table he takes the bread, says "words of blessing", divides it and gives it (24:30). Genesis 3:6 also has a taking and a giving, and the Greek "took" is the same word in both. What the Genesis 3 taking brings is two curses (3:14, 3:17) and bread got by "the hard work of your hands" (3:19). Here the bread comes with a blessing, at a table, and nothing is said about work. The blessing comes round again at the end of the chapter, when he lifts his hands and gives them a blessing (24:50). In Genesis 1 the blessing is given to the sea creatures (1:22) and then to the man (1:28), so the chapter ends where the first scene began.

Out, and back the same hour

They went out of Jerusalem (24:13) and "that very hour" went back (24:33). In Genesis 3 the man is sent out (3:23-24). The same movement shows in the younger son on the Luke and John page: out, then in. Jerusalem is "foundation of peace", and the first words in the room are "Peace be with you!" (24:36). When they arrive they are told "the Lord has truly come back to life again, and Simon has seen him" (24:34). Simon is "hearing", and the walkers' own story ran in that order: he talked on the road and their hearts burned (24:32), then they saw him at the table (24:31). They finish by telling how "when he gave them bread, they had knowledge of him" (24:35).

What comes next in the chapter

He stands among them and says "Peace be with you!" (24:36). They think they see a spirit, so he lets them see his hands and feet, asks "Have you any food here?" and takes a bit of cooked fish "before their eyes" (24:41-43). Then "he made the holy Writings clear to their minds" (24:45), which is the same verb as the eyes. By the end of the chapter the eyes, the Writings and the minds have all been opened, and the chapter closes on a blessing, worship and praise in the Temple (24:50-53).

The words checked, in one place

  • Eyes were open (dianoigō, G1272): Strong's gives "to open thoroughly, literally (as a first-born) or figuratively (to expound)". It is used eight times in the New Testament: Mark 7:34, Mark 7:35, Luke 2:23, Luke 24:31, Luke 24:32, Luke 24:45, Acts 16:14 and Acts 17:3. The Septuagint text of Genesis 3:5 (dianoichthēsontai) and 3:7 (diēnoichthēsan) uses the same verb, and Luke 24:31 has the identical form to Genesis 3:7. Luke 24:27 and 24:16 are not on the list, so a different Greek word is behind "made clear" in 24:27 and "not open" in 24:16, even though the BBE's English makes 24:16 look like the same word as 24:31.
  • Took (lambanō, G2983): in the Septuagint of Genesis 3:6 and in Luke 24:30.
  • Emmaus (Emmaous, G1695): used once in the New Testament, at Luke 24:13. Thayer gives "warm baths". Strong's gives it as of Hebrew origin and compares H3222, "a warm spring".
  • Cleopas (G2810): used once, at Luke 24:18. Thayer gives "of a renowned father", and Strong's has it from Kleopatros, a compound of "renown" (G2811, "renown (as if being called)") and "father" (G3962).
  • Nazareth (G3478): "the guarded one", as the Blue Letter Bible gives it.
  • Israel (G2474): "of Hebrew origin" (H3478). Strong's H3478 gives "he will rule as God", from H8280 and H410, and BDB gives "God prevails". The page uses "He shall prevail", as on the other pages.
  • Moses (G3475): "of Hebrew origin" (H4872). Strong's H4872 gives "drawing out (of the water), i.e. rescued", from H4871, "to pull out".
  • Simon (G4613): "of Hebrew origin" (H8095). Strong's H8095 gives "hearing", from H8085.
  • Had knowledge of him (epiginōskō, G1921): the verb in both Luke 24:16 (eyes shut) and Luke 24:31 (eyes open), so the shut eyes and the open eyes turn on one word. Strong's has it "from G1909 and G1097". The Septuagint of Genesis 3:7 has the plain verb G1097 for "knew" (egnōsan).
  • Christ (Christos, G5547): "anointed", from G5548.
  • Jerusalem (H3389): Strong's gives "foundation of peace".
  • Jesus: "Existing One is salvation", as on the other pages.

Where this fits in the course

A man claims an identity and the court makes it real (Lesson 6). At Emmaus the identity is already beside the walkers, and what they say about the scene is lack. Rule One says what is planted gives back only its own kind (Lesson 7). The story does not argue with the lack. It plants something else: first the Writings opened, then the bread with its blessing, and the eyes follow. The food in Genesis 3 comes by the hands (3:19), and here it comes by the table (24:30). The summary of how Luke puts the Genesis scene back is on the Luke and John page.

A question to ask of any story: when the eyes open, what do they open onto, the lack or the name?

Remember: the eyes are shut while the talk is all lack. The Writings open on the road and the hearts burn. At the table the bread is taken, blessed, broken and given, and the eyes open with the same verb as Genesis 3:7, this time on the identity that was walking beside them. He goes from their view, and the knowing goes back to Jerusalem that same hour.
