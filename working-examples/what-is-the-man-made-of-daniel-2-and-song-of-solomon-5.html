A Man Made of the Genesis Scene: Daniel 2 and Song of Solomon 5

Two different books describe a man piece by piece. In Daniel 2 the king sees a statue. In Song of Solomon 5 a woman describes her loved one from head to foot. Both lists are built from the same kind of material: gold, stone, earth and metal in one, and gold, stone, birds, water, plants and trees in the other. Those are the pieces of the Genesis scene. So the man in each story is the Genesis scene put together as a body.

Why would a man be made of the scene?

Genesis does it first. The man is made out of the same material as the scene around him:

And the Lord God made man from the dust of the earth, breathing into him the breath of life: and man became a living soul. — Genesis 2:7 (BBE)
And from the earth the Lord God made every beast of the field and every bird of the air, and took them to the man to see what names he would give them. — Genesis 2:19 (BBE)

The man, the beasts and the birds all come "from the earth". So the man and the scene are one material from the start. In this course, man is the one who claims an identity (Lesson 6), and the scene is the state that identity stands in. When a later book describes a man piece by piece, the pieces tell you which state is being claimed, in the same way a name does in Lesson 9. Genesis never states this rule outright. It shows the man made from the scene's material, and Daniel and the Song then describe a man in that same material.

The statue in Daniel 2

As for this image, its head was made of the best gold, its breast and its arms were of silver, its middle and its sides were of brass, Its legs of iron, its feet were in part of iron and in part of potter's earth. — Daniel 2:32-33 (BBE)

The head is named in the story itself: "you are the head of gold" (Daniel 2:38). The text then says the king was given rule "over them all", meaning the beasts of the field and the birds of heaven. That is the rule given to the man in Genesis 1:

And God said, Let us make man in our image, like us: and let him have rule over the fish of the sea and over the birds of the air and over the cattle and over all the earth and over every living thing which goes flat on the earth. — Genesis 1:26 (BBE)

So the top of the statue is the stand of Genesis 1:26, a man in an image with rule over the creatures. The story uses the word "image" for the statue too (Daniel 2:31). Gold also comes from the Genesis scene. It is named in the ground of Eden before anything goes wrong:

The name of the first is Pishon, which goes round about all the land of Havilah where there is gold. And the gold of that land is good: there is bdellium and the onyx stone. — Genesis 2:11-12 (BBE)

Eden means "pleasure" (Strong's H5731), and the gold of that ground is called good. The head of the statue is the best of the original scene.

Going down the body, the material gets harder and then breaks

The next parts are silver, brass and iron. Brass and iron turn up in Genesis in one place, in the line of Cain:

And Zillah gave birth to Tubal-cain, who is the father of every maker of cutting instruments of brass and iron. — Genesis 4:22 (BBE)

I have not tied silver to Genesis 1 to 4, so I leave it as a step on the way down. The legs are iron, the metal of tools that cut. The feet are the last part, and they are iron mixed with the earth that potters use. In Genesis 2:7 the man is made "from the dust of the earth". So the feet bring the man's own material back into the picture, now mixed with iron.

The mixing is what the story stresses. Here is the verse:

And as you saw the iron mixed with earth, they will give their daughters to one another as wives: but they will not be united one with another, even as iron is not mixed with earth. — Daniel 2:43 (BBE)

"Not united" is the Aramaic word debaq (Strong's H1693). It occurs once in the whole Bible, here. Its Strong's entry says it corresponds to the Hebrew dabaq (H1692), the word Genesis 2:24 uses for the man being "joined to his wife". So the same rule from Lesson 8 is being broken in the feet. Wives are given, but nothing is joined. And the seed rule from Lesson 7 is in view too: the pieces will not mix, "even as iron is not mixed with earth". Each is after its own kind.

What happens to the statue

While you were looking at it, a stone was cut out, but not by hands, and it gave the image a blow on its feet, which were of iron and earth, and they were broken in bits. — Daniel 2:34 (BBE)

The blow lands on the feet, the part where the pieces would not join. Then everything goes to dust: the iron, the earth, the brass, the silver and the gold (Daniel 2:35). The statue ends as the same dust the man was made from in Genesis 2:7. The stone becomes "a great mountain, covering all the earth". In Genesis 1 the dry land is the ground that appears before anything is put on it. So the story ends with ground again, and the body of mixed parts is gone.

Time is in the picture as well. Daniel says "times and years are changed" (2:21), and Genesis 1:14 says the lights are made "for marking the changes of the year". The statue is the scene of Genesis 1 seen over time, and the changes are what break it down.

The loved one in Song of Solomon 5

The woman has just lost him at the door (5:6). The women ask what makes him special, and she answers by building him out loud:

His head is as the most delicate gold; his hair is thick, and black as a raven. His eyes are as the eyes of doves by the water streams, washed with milk, and rightly placed. His face is as beds of spices, giving out perfumes of every sort; his lips like lilies, dropping liquid myrrh. — Song of Solomon 5:11-13 (BBE)
His hands are as rings of gold ornamented with beryl-stones; his body is as a smooth plate of ivory covered with sapphires. His legs are as pillars of stone on a base of delicate gold; his looks are as Lebanon, beautiful as the cedar-tree. — Song of Solomon 5:14-15 (BBE)

Compare the pieces with Genesis:

  • Gold and stone: the head, hands and the base under the legs are gold, and the legs are stone. These are the materials of the good land in Genesis 2:11-12.
  • Birds and water: hair like a raven and eyes like doves by the water streams. Genesis 1:20 fills the waters with living things and puts the birds over the earth.
  • Plants: spices, lilies, myrrh and the cedar-tree. Genesis 1:11-12 has plants and trees, each producing seed "of its sort".
  • Mountain: "his looks are as Lebanon". Lebanon means "white mountain" or "whiteness" (Strong's H3844). The Song has just called him "white and red" (5:10). That is a different Hebrew word for white, so I am not saying the two are linked by a word, only that the place name and the colour agree.

Look at where the gold sits. In Daniel the gold is only at the head and everything below it is worse. In the Song the gold is at the head, in the hands, and under the legs, so the stone legs stand on gold. Nothing in his list is brass, iron or clay. The Daniel statue is built from the scene and then mixed with the Cain metals. The Song man is built from the scene and kept as it was. The phrase "rightly placed" (5:12) says the same: every piece is in its place.

What changed from Genesis 1

Lesson 5 says every story is the Genesis 1 picture, changed. Here the picture is the man himself, so each part of the body can be set against the part of Genesis 1 it matches. The statue and the loved one use the same pieces. What differs is what happens to them.

So what changed is the body. In Daniel the pieces start with the gold of Eden and the rule over the creatures, take on the metals of Genesis 4, and end as iron mixed with earth that will not join. The blow comes at the feet, everything becomes dust, and what is left is a mountain, which is ground. In the Song the pieces stay in the Genesis 1 and 2 materials and are each in place, but the scene around them is the night, a door, and a man who has gone (5:6). The creatures and the rule are not in it, and no one calls him good. Both stories give you the original pieces. They differ on whether the pieces hold together.

Which part moved?

This is the stand, the body the identity stands in, from Lesson 5. Both stories describe the body of the man as the Genesis scene itself, piece by piece. The difference is what the pieces are:

  • Daniel 2: the body starts with the gold of Eden and rule over the creatures, passes through the metals of Genesis 4, and ends in iron mixed with earth that will not join. It is broken at the feet and goes back to dust, and the ground (the mountain) is what is left.
  • Song of Solomon 5: the body keeps the Genesis 1 and 2 pieces: gold, stone, birds, water, spices, lilies, the cedar. Each is rightly placed and the man stands on gold.

Three questions for any story:

  1. What is the ground, and what does its name mean?
  2. Who is standing on it, and what is the body made of?
  3. What has changed from the original picture in Genesis 1?

Remember: a man described in pieces is the Genesis scene put together as a body. Check what each piece is made from, and whether the pieces join.
