An Alternative Reading of the Bible: Common Questions, Answered From the Text

A different reading from the traditional explanations, in plain English and checked against the verse and Strong's: why "God" is plural in Genesis 1, what "after its kind" means, who the Nephilim are, why Cain's offering was rejected, why Abraham was asked to sacrifice Isaac, why Pharaoh's heart was hardened, what "render unto Caesar" means, who the serpent in Genesis 3 is, why Paul does what he hates in Romans 7, what "ask and it will be given" means, and the stories of Ruth, Job, Joseph, Moses and Jacob.

This is not the usual reading. If you are looking for an alternative Bible interpretation, a symbolic or psychological reading, the hidden meaning behind Bible names, or what the story says beyond the traditional explanation, start here. Most explanations treat the Bible as history, morals or religion, and many start from a theory and skip the plot. This course keeps to what the text itself shows. Every story has the same three parts: someone present, a name or identity that person takes on, and judges whose ruling follows it. Genesis 1 sets those parts out first, and the course follows them from story to story. Because Genesis 1 makes man the one who is made in the judges' own image, the three parts are read as yours: you, the identity you claim, and the judges that make it real. If you have come from Neville Goddard's teaching: he pointed to what the Bible says, but he also mixed in some traditional theology the text does not support. Is this an interpretation, or is it what the text says?

It is called an alternative reading because it differs from the usual explanations. It is not an alternative to the text. Nothing is brought in from outside, because the Bible sets up its own labels and then keeps using them the same way.

Genesis 1 does it first. Each thing is named as it is made:

Naming the light, Day, and the dark, Night. — Genesis 1:5 (BBE)

The arch is named Heaven (Genesis 1:8). The dry land is named Earth and the gathered waters are named Seas (Genesis 1:10). The opening chapter is a word list the text writes for itself, and every later story is built from those words.

Babel does it next. A city is named, and the reason is given in the same verse:

So it was named Babel, because there the Lord took away the sense of all languages and from there the Lord sent them away over all the face of the earth. — Genesis 11:9 (BBE)

Babel (Strong's H894) comes from balal (H1101), "to mix, to confuse." The name is the event. When Babylon comes up again, the story is back in the state the name describes, as in Jeremiah 29. The rule is also stated outright inside a story, in 1 Samuel 25: "as his name is, so is he."

So the method here is simply to keep the plot and keep the labels. Read each name by the meaning the text gives it, or by Strong's where the text does not spell it out, and watch the story follow it. How to check it.

Common Bible questions, answered from the text

  • Where do I start reading the Bible if I don't believe?
    A plain way in that needs no belief, with four steps to check everything yourself.
  • What does 'after its kind' mean in Genesis 1:11?
    A claim gives back only what it is.
  • What is Genesis 2:24 (leave and cleave) really saying?
    Step away from the old state and join the new identity.
  • Is the Bible's male and female about gender?
    No. Man is you, the one claiming. Woman is the identity you take on. Both are in the reader, whatever your gender.
  • Do Bible names and places have meanings that tell the story?
    Yes. Each is a code for the state being lived in. Read the meaning, not just the label.
  • Where is Edom in the Bible?
    Edom means "red". It is Esau, then the land he settles: Seir, "hairy".
  • Why is 'God' plural in Genesis 1?
    The Hebrew word Elohim is plural: the judges and rulers, acting as one. That is why the text says 'he'.
  • What does YHVH mean?
    'The existing One' (Strong's H3068): you, aware right now. When deliberately introspective of the self.
  • What does I AM mean in the Bible?
    The identity you choose and claim.
  • What is Genesis 1 really about?
    The scene is built first, then the identity is placed in it. Every later story is that picture, changed.
  • What is the story of Ruth really about?
    With the name meanings put in place, Ruth 1 shows the Genesis 2:24 rule at work.
  • What is the story of Job really about?
    Job means "hated". He acts out a fear, wrecks the scene Genesis 1 built, and the fear is what arrives.
  • Why did God try to kill Moses in Exodus 4:24?
    The verse gives no reason. Moses means "drawn out", Egypt means "double straits", and the cut is the mark on the seed.
  • Did Jesus save the people out of Egypt in Jude 5?
    Jude 5 says "the Lord" in one reading and "Jesus" in the other, and Moses is not named.
  • What is the story of Joseph really about?
    Down, held, brought out: pit to palace.
  • Who are the Nephilim in Genesis 6:4?
    Not a species. The bully identity that forms when your judging side starts choosing by looks.
  • What was the sin of Sodom and Gomorrah in Genesis 19?
    The story gives its own reasons, and the town's name tells you the state it was in.
  • Why did God ask Abraham to sacrifice Isaac?
    A test of what you hold: Abraham is the identity, Isaac (laughter) is the proof, and the proof is spared.
  • Why did God reject Cain's offering?
    Cain means possession, Abel means breath, and Eden means pleasure. Cain brings the fruit of ground already cursed, so his offering brings no pleasure.
  • Why did God harden Pharaoh's heart?
    The heart is the mind, and a hard heart is one that has not changed. The Lord hardening it and Pharaoh hardening it are one event seen from two sides.
  • Why did Balaam's donkey talk in Numbers 22?
    A people the Judges have already blessed cannot be cursed, and once the eyes are opened the mouth can say only the blessing.
  • What did Jesus mean by 'render unto Caesar'?
    He changes the question from the tax to whose image is on it, and the image goes back to Genesis 1:26-27.
  • What did Jesus mean by 'foxes have holes' in Matthew 8:20?
    The voices in you promise, and the answer is a list of places.
  • Why did Jacob put sticks in front of the flock?
    Laban removes every marked animal, and the flock still bears marked young. What is named stands, even with the proof taken away.
  • Who were Nabal and Abigail in 1 Samuel 25?
    The text gives the rule itself: "as his name is, so is he." Nabal means fool, Abigail means source of joy, David means beloved, and Source of Joy steps between Beloved and his four hundred swords.
  • What does Jeremiah 29:11 really mean?
    It is one line in a letter to people in Babylon. The peace is asked for first, the years are counted, and the thoughts are the same two words that fill Genesis 6:5 with evil.
  • What does the Song of Solomon mean?
    A woman keeps other people's vineyards until she is called to leave and join, as Genesis 2:23-24 describes.
  • Who is the serpent in Genesis 3?
    A made creature the man was meant to rule. He listens to it instead, and the fruit is judging by how things look.
  • Why do I do what I hate in Romans 7:15?
    The wish is a preference, and the doing is what the claim you hold works out. The "I" stands under two rulings, and the passage ends with both still named.
  • How do I read the Proverbs?
    The Proverbs name two types, the one with sense and the fool. Nabal's story shows both in one house, and Matthew sets the wise against the foolish with the same pair of words.
  • What does "ask and it will be given" mean?
    Asking is filing a claim about who you are, and the court gives after its kind. A fish is never swapped for a snake, and the one asking is the identity placed over the scene.
  • Does the Bible contradict itself?
    A clashing pair is one story told from two places in the plot.
  • What is the book of Esther about?
    A sealed law cannot be changed, so the verdict against a whole people is answered by a second writing under the same seal, and the hidden identity has to be put on record first.
  • What do 'Jew', 'Gentile' and the '-ites' mean in the Bible?
    Jew means belonging to Praise. Gentile means nation, and Israel is one. An -ite belongs to the name in front of it, and the story shows the name.
  • What does the Bible say about money?
    Twelve money verses read against Genesis 1, where the food is already in the scene. The heart fixed on getting is the problem, and what is put in comes back after its kind.
  • What is the living God in the Bible?
    The Hebrew behind "living" is chay, the word for what is green, flowing and alive, and the name Eve carries it. The judges rule for the identity that is alive now, held as "I am".
  • What does holy mean in the Bible?
    Holy means set apart. The first thing set apart is a day, in Genesis 2:3, and the judges are the ones who set it apart.
  • Who told you that you were naked in Genesis 3:11?
    The Hebrew for naked in 2:25 and for wiser in 3:1 is one word family: bare, then shrewd. After the shrewd voice speaks the text shows covering and hiding, and the court asks where the knowledge came from.
  • Why was the kingdom torn from Solomon in 1 Kings 11?
    A prophet tears a new robe into twelve, and the Hebrew uses the same verb, tear, for the robe and for the kingdom taken out of Solomon's hand. The heart joined to many is not whole, and the kingdom comes back in pieces after its kind.
  • Who was Mary Magdalene in John 20?
    Rebellion, built up like a tower, stays in the garden after the others go home. It is the Eve scene played backwards: the same title Woman, the same question, a hand held back, and a word carried the other way.
  • What does John 5:39 mean?
    The Writings give witness and are not the verdict. The voice, the identity and the witnesses are the Genesis 1 split, set in a courtroom.
  • What does the floating axe head mean?
    Elisha asks only where, throws one stick, and the iron floats. The Hebrew calls the iron "asked-for", the same word as the name Saul, and the man who asked is the one told to take it.
  • What do the rods and sticks in the Bible mean?
    A stick is a piece of a tree, and the tree gives after its kind. Jacob's rods, Moses' rod, Aaron's budding rod and Elisha's stick work where they are set in the place of the trouble and match what was named. They fail when swung in anger or used instead of the word.
  • What do the Greek words in Revelation 22:12-21 mean?
    The ending reads like the close of a court case: wages given back for work, a speaker who is the beginning and the rule, robes washed and a right over the tree, and a record that nothing may be added to or taken from.
  • What do the fig leaves and the skins in Genesis 3 mean?
    Two coverings: leaves the man and woman sew, and skins the Judges make.
  • What do 'Son of God' and 'Son of man' mean?
    One person with two sides in Genesis, two groups in Genesis 6, and one person again in Luke 3:38.
  • Do I need to accept this on trust?
    No. Every step can be checked against the verse and Strong's.

Or follow the whole course, in order

  1. The Big Idea. The Bible works like a courtroom. You make a claim about who you are, and the judges rule to match it.
  2. The Three Parts. Every story runs on three parts: YHVH, I AM and Elohim. Learn these and the rest is easy.
  3. Why 'God' Is Plural. Where your Bible says God, the Hebrew is usually Elohim, a plural word. Read it as the judges and rulers inside you.
  4. Genesis 1 Is the Word List. The seven days of creation fix the words the whole Bible uses.
  5. The Original Plot. Genesis 1 is the master picture. Every later story is that picture, changed.
  6. Man Is the One Who Claims. In this reading, man is the one who claims an identity and has it made real.
  7. Rule One: After Its Kind. What is planted gives back only what it is.
  8. Rule Two: Leave and Join. To take on a new identity you step away from the old state and join the new one (Genesis 2:24).
  9. Names and Places Are Codes. In the Bible a name tells you what the state is like before the story starts. Read the meaning, not just the title.
  10. Worked Example: Edom, Esau and Seir. Read Genesis 25 and 36 twice: first as written, then with the name and place meanings put in. Red stands on hairy ground.
  11. Worked Example: Ruth 1. Read Ruth 1 twice: first as written, then with the name and place meanings put in.
  12. Worked Example: Job. Read Job twice: first as written, then with the name meanings put in. A man named Hated wrecks the scene Genesis 1 built.
  13. The Pattern Every Story Follows. Most stories run three steps: down, held, brought out. Joseph shows it from pit to palace.
  14. Worked Example: Moses, Exodus 4:24. Read the scene twice: first as written, then with the name and place meanings put in. A name Egypt gave him walks back into Egypt, and the seed in his house is unmarked.
  15. Worked Example: The Nephilim, Genesis 6:1-4. Read the passage twice: first as written, then with the meanings put in. The judging part of you chooses by looks, and a bully identity forms.
  16. Worked Example: Abraham and Isaac, Genesis 22. Read the passage twice: first as written, then with the meanings put in. Two laughs name the child, and the test is whether you hold the identity when the proof is on the altar.
  17. Worked Example: Cain and Abel, Genesis 4. Read the passage twice: first as written, then with the meanings put in. Cain means possession, Abel means breath. Cain brings the fruit of ground already cursed, so his offering brings no pleasure, and the collapse multiplies after its kind.
  18. Worked Example: Pharaoh's Heart, Exodus 7-14. Read the passage twice: first as written, then with the meanings put in. The heart is the mind, and a hard heart is a mind that has not changed.
  19. Worked Example: Render Unto Caesar, Matthew 22:15-22. Read the passage twice: first as written, then with the meanings put in. The separatists ask for a cut with two doors, and the answer is whose image.
  20. Worked Example: Jacob and the Rods, Genesis 30-31. Read the passage twice: first as written, then with the meanings put in. The proof is taken away, the claim is held where the flock drinks, and the flock follows it.
  21. Worked Example: Nabal and Abigail, 1 Samuel 25. Read the passage twice: first as written, then with the meanings put in. A fool stands in a fruitful field, Beloved comes up with four hundred swords, and Source of Joy steps between them.
  22. Worked Example: Jeremiah 29:11. Read the passage twice: first as written, then with the meanings put in. Peace is held in a city named Confusion, seventy years are counted, and the thoughts are the same two words that fill Genesis 6:5 with evil.
  23. Worked Example: The Serpent, Genesis 3. Read the passage twice: first as written, then with the meanings put in. The man was made to rule the creatures, here he answers one, and the fruit is judging by looks.
  24. Worked Example: Romans 7:15. Read the passage twice: first as written, then with the meanings put in. The wish is a choice and the doing is worked out by the claim held, the same verb that worked desire in at verse 8 does the work at verse 15, and the court's rule is not the fault.
  25. Worked Example: How to Read the Proverbs. Read the proverbs next to the story of Nabal. The fool is a full scene with nothing holding it, and Matthew 7 and 25 use the same pair, wise and foolish.
  26. Worked Example: Ask, Seek, Knock, Matthew 7:7-11. Read the passage twice: first as written, then with the meanings put in. Asking is filing a claim, the court gives after its kind, and a scene can change but a fish is never swapped for a snake.

The big picture in 60 seconds

  1. YHVH (the LORD) is you, aware right now. The reader when introspective
  2. I AM is the identity you claim.
  3. Elohim are the judges and rulers inside you. They make the identity you hold become real.

Then use the cheat sheet and its six-step method on any passage you like. In the BBE, 'the Lord' stands for YHVH and 'God' stands for Elohim.

More worked examples

Extra passages read the same way, outside the numbered course. Each one shows the verse, the word meanings and the Genesis 1 link, so you can check it yourself.

  • What did Jesus mean by 'foxes have holes'? Matthew 8:18-22
    The voices in you promise, and the answer is a list of places. The scene then runs Genesis 1 in order, from the deep to the man, and the chapter names him as the one made in the Judges' image.
  • What does the Song of Solomon mean? Song of Solomon 1-2
    A dark woman keeps other people's vineyards, a shepherd calls her to leave, and the scene is built from the Genesis 1 list. It names, leaves and joins as Genesis 2:23-24 does, then holds before the fruit.
  • Did Jesus save the people out of Egypt? Jude 5
    Jude 5 says "the Lord" in one reading and "Jesus" in the other, and Moses is not named. The Lord is the existing One, and Jesus is the name that says the existing One saves, so both readings name one subject taking a people out of the double straits.
  • Does the Bible contradict itself? Three famous examples
    A clashing pair is one story told from two places in the plot. Genesis 1 and 2, Pharaoh's heart, and 1 Samuel 15 are each read with three questions: whose side is it told from, which name is used, and is the mind changing or holding.
  • What was the sin of Sodom and Gomorrah? Genesis 19
    The story gives its own reasons in order: the outcry, the court going down to look, the whole town round the door, and the judge refused. Read with the name meanings, the town is a burning state, and the scene is taken down through the same categories Genesis 1 used to build it.
  • Why did Balaam's donkey talk? Numbers 22
    A king named Waster hires a voice named Not-of-the-people to curse a people the Judges have already blessed, the same first word as Genesis 1:28. The patient she-ass sees the sword in the road three times, and once the eyes are opened the mouth can say only the blessing.
  • What is the book of Esther about? Esther 1-10
    The book never names the court, but it shows one: a law that cannot be changed and a seal. Haman, 'Magnificent', is lifted up and files a verdict against the Praised-line, Esther, 'star', keeps her people off the record until she declares them, and a second writing under the same seal turns the first back on the man who made it.
  • What do Jew, Gentile and the -ites mean? Genesis 19, Esther 8, Romans 2, Judges 3
    Jew is built from the name Praise, and you can become one. A Gentile is a nation, and Israel is called one. Each -ite belongs to a name, and the story of each name shows what it means: Moab, Ammon, Edom, the Kenite, the Jebusite and more.
  • What does the Bible say about money? 12 verses in context
    Genesis 1 supplies the food before the man appears, so every money verse is that scene changed. 1 Timothy 6:10, Matthew 6:24, Malachi 3:10, Luke 6:38, Mark 10:25 and seven more are each read with three questions: what is the ground, who is standing on it, and what is the court enforcing after its kind.
  • What is the living God? Joshua 3:10
    El chay: God and living. Chay is the Genesis word for what is green, flowing and alive, and Eve, "life-giver", carries it. "God" is the judges, and they rule for the identity alive now, held as "I am" rather than "I was".
  • What does holy mean? Genesis 2:3, Exodus 3:5, Leviticus 19:2
    Holy means set apart. The judges bless the seventh day and set it apart from the work, a place at Horeb, "waste", is holy where the "I am" is said, and the people are told to be set apart as the judges are.
  • What do the ram and the goat mean? Daniel 8
    The ram and the goat are two kinds from one house, brothers in Genesis 10:2, and each makes itself great, which in Genesis 1 only the judges do. The goat never touches the ground, a horn pulls down the army the judges finished, and the vision ends on the judges' own day-count, evenings and mornings. Its last word, tsadaq, means to be made right in a courtroom sense.
  • Who carried the cross, Jesus or Simon of Cyrene? John 19, Matthew 27, Mark 15, Luke 23
    John says Jesus went out with his cross on him, and the other three say a man named Simon, "hearing", was made to take it after him. The page keeps both. The scene is built from Genesis 3: a tree carried out, thorns, a hearing brought in from the field, Dead Man's Head and a garden beside it. Each gospel tells the walk the way its own name says, and John, the gracious one, is where nobody is pressed in.
  • What does Luke 14:1-6 mean? The Sabbath and the man made well
    A ruler's table on the Sabbath, and the voices that read the statute keep still. A man with water in him, named for the water in the Greek, is cured and freed by the statute the judges fixed on day three of Genesis 1, and the question about the ox in the water-hole gets no answer, because the answer is their own practice.
  • Why does the temple curtain tear? Luke 23:45
    A garden is promised, the land goes dark, the light set in the arch fails, and the curtain is parted. The Hebrew word for the veil's dividing is the one Genesis 1:6 uses for the arch, and the veil carries the winged ones that Genesis 3:24 puts at Eden to keep the way. John is the odd one out: no curtain, the temple already the body, and nothing torn.
  • Who told you that you were naked? Genesis 2:25 to 3:11
    The man and his wife are bare (arummim, H6174) and the snake is shrewd (arum, H6175), one word family a verse apart. After the shrewd voice speaks the text shows covering and hiding, the court asks who gave the knowledge, and the word sin does not appear until Cain's door in Genesis 4:7.
  • Why was the kingdom torn from Solomon? 1 Kings 11
    The court rules first, then a prophet tears a new robe into twelve. The Hebrew verb for the robe and for the kingdom is one word, qara, "tear", and the BBE hides it as "by force". The heart joined to many, as in Genesis 2:24, is not whole, and ten pieces go to the man over the house of Joseph.
  • Who was Mary Magdalene? John 20:1-18
    Mary is Miriam, "rebellion", and Magdalene is "a tower". Hearing and Stone go home while she stays in the garden. Woman, the gardener, a question, a hand held back, and a word carried to the brothers each match a line of Genesis 2-3, in the reverse direction.
  • What does John 5:39 mean? "You search the scriptures"
    The verse does not say stop searching. The Writings give witness, one witness among five, and the chapter splits as Genesis 1 does: a voice that speaks, an identity given the judging, and a crowd who hold the evidence and have no desire to come.
  • What does the floating axe head mean? 2 Kings 6:1-7
    A borrowed iron blade is lost in the Descender, and Elisha asks only where. The Hebrew for borrowed is "asked-for", spelled like the name Saul, so the page reads it beside the king who was asked for and the iron that Israel had to take to others. One cut stick lifts the iron, and the hand that asked takes it back.
  • What do the rods and sticks in the Bible mean? Genesis 30, Exodus 4 and 7, Numbers 17, 2 Kings 6
    Jacob's peeled rods, Moses' rod that becomes a snake, Aaron's rod that buds and Elisha's stick are read together as pieces of the tree after its kind. The rule is tested against Numbers 20 and 22, Genesis 38 and 1 Samuel 17, and the page says where the first guess failed and was changed.
  • What do the Greek words in Revelation 22:12-21 mean? The last ten verses of the Bible
    Misthos and apodidomi pay back each one's work, archē is both "beginning" and "rule", genos is the "kind" of "after its kind", and "I come" and "Come" are one verb. The page lines the ending up against Genesis 1 to 3 and says which links are word matches and which are only echoes.
  • What do the fig leaves and the skins in Genesis 3 mean? Genesis 3:7 and 3:21
    Two coverings are made in Genesis 3. The man and the woman sew leaves, and the Existing One of the Judges makes skins and clothes them. The leaves come from the one part of the plant Genesis 1 never defines.
  • What do 'Son of God' and 'Son of man' mean? Genesis 1 to Luke 3
    Genesis keeps the God side and the man side in one person, Genesis 6 splits them into two groups, and Luke 3:38 puts them back on one person: Adam, the son of God.
