What Did Jesus Mean by 'Render Unto Caesar'? Matthew 22:15-22

Short answer: Jesus does not say yes or no to the tax. He asks whose image is on the coin. They say Caesar's, so the coin goes back to Caesar. The same test then applies to the other half: what carries the image of the Judges (Genesis 1:26-27) is the Judges'.

"Render unto Caesar the things that are Caesar's" is Matthew 22:21, and the same scene is in Mark 12:13-17 and Luke 20:20-26. People ask whether Jesus is telling them to pay tax, or to split life between two masters. In this course the answer is in the question he asks instead of the one he is given: whose image is on it? Read the passage first, then read it again with the meanings put in.

The meanings used

  • Pharisees (pharisaios, G5330): "a separatist, i.e. exclusively religious", from the Hebrew word for "to separate".
  • Herodians (hērōdianoi, G2265): "partisans of Herod".
  • Jesus (Iesous, G2424): from the Hebrew Yehoshua, "Jehovah is salvation". Jehovah is YHVH, "the existing One" (Lesson 2), so the name reads Existing-One-is-salvation.
  • Caesar (kaisar, G2541): a title of the Roman emperor, like Pharaoh, not a personal name. Thayer's entry, shown on Blue Letter Bible, glosses it "severed".
  • False ones (hypokrites, G5273): "an actor under an assumed character".
  • God (theos, G2316): "a deity", and also "figuratively, a magistrate". It is the word the Greek Genesis uses for the Judges in Genesis 1:26 (Lesson 3).
  • Image (eikon, G1504): "a likeness, i.e. (literally) statue, profile, or (figuratively) representation, resemblance". It is the same word the Greek Genesis uses in 1:26-27.
  • Inscription (epigraphe, G1923): "an inscription, title". The BBE renders it "name" in 22:20.

One note on "give": "render" is the KJV's word. The BBE says "give" both in the question (22:17) and in the answer (22:21), but the Greek has two words. The question uses the plain word for give (dounai, G1325). The answer uses apodidomi (G591), "to give away, i.e. up, over, back", the word used for paying back what is owed (Matthew 18:30; Luke 12:59). So the question is about giving, and the answer is about giving back.

How to read it, step by step

  1. The names set the scene. The Pharisees are "separatists", they bring Herod's partisans, and the question is about tax to Caesar, "Severed". Everything in it is cut in two: yes or no, Caesar or God. "Is it right to give tax to Caesar, or not?" (22:17, BBE). This is the next attempt by the same people who, in the chapter before, "had a desire to take him" (21:46, BBE). The one they ask is Jesus, "Jehovah is salvation", and Jehovah is YHVH, the Existing One, you aware right now. So the scene acts out his name: the Existing One is salvation, the way out of a cut that has only two doors.
  2. Actors in an assumed character. They flatter him first: "we see that you are true" and you "have no fear of anyone" (22:16, BBE). Jesus "saw their trick" and calls them "false ones" (22:18, BBE). The Greek word means "an actor under an assumed character". They come in the character of admiring disciples and hold a different aim. So the scene begins with a mind showing one identity while holding another.
  3. He changes the question. He does not say yes or no. "Let me see the tax money. And they gave him a penny" (22:19, BBE). He does not produce the coin. They do. Then he asks a different kind of question: "Whose is this image and name on it?" (22:20, BBE). They asked whether to give, which is a question about an act. He asks whose, which is a question about what is stamped on a thing.
  4. The image decides. "They say to him, Caesar's" (22:21, BBE). They answer it themselves. The coin carries Caesar's image, so it is Caesar's, and the answer is "give back", not "give". Giving back what carries the owner's image is not losing anything. Then the same test on the other half: "and to God the things which are God's" (22:21, BBE). The text does not list them, but it has just given the test: what carries whose image? The first image in the Bible is in Genesis 1: "Let us make man in our image" (Genesis 1:26, BBE). So what carries the image of the Judges is the Judges'. In this course that is you, the one made in the image, and the Judges are the standing rulers of your own mind. See the Bible's court language.
  5. Why they went away. "And hearing it, they were full of wonder, and went away from him" (22:22, BBE). They came "to take him" (22:15, BBE) and they "went away from him" (22:22, BBE). His answer is behind neither door of their question, because it moves the question from the tax to the image. That is the name acted out: the Existing One is salvation, and the cut with two doors does not take him.

Who was asking, and why it was a trap

The text says it plainly. The Pharisees "had a meeting to see how they might make use of his words to take him" (22:15, BBE). They send their disciples with Herod's partisans, open with praise, and then put a question with two doors: "Is it right to give tax to Caesar, or not?" (22:17, BBE). Jesus "saw their trick" (22:18, BBE). Either answer would give them something to use, so he answers neither door. The text names the coin as a denarius and says only that the image is "Caesar's" (22:21, BBE). It does not say which Caesar.

Is "render unto Caesar" about paying taxes?

The question was about tax, but he never says "pay it" and never says "do not pay it." Some readers take that as an order to pay tax, and others say the passage is not about tax at all.

Is it about church and state?

The phrase is often quoted as a rule for church and state, but the passage names no church and no state. It names a coin, an image, Caesar, and "God", which is the Judges (theos, "a magistrate").

What the usual reading misses

  1. The Greek has two words. The question uses the plain word for give (dounai, G1325). The answer uses apodidomi (G591), "give back". Luke 20:25 and Mark 12:17 use the same word for the answer (apodote, G591), and Mark 12:14 uses the plain word for give (dounai) in the question.
  2. The image is the Genesis 1:26 word. The Greek eikon (G1504) is the word the Greek Genesis uses for "image" in 1:26-27, and Mark 12:16 and Luke 20:24 use it for the image on the coin.
  3. The coin carries a name as well as an image. The BBE says "image and name" (22:20). The Greek word, epigraphe, is used only five times in the New Testament: three times of this coin (Matthew 22:20; Mark 12:16; Luke 20:24) and twice of the writing put over Jesus on the cross (Mark 15:26; Luke 23:38). A name tells the state a thing belongs to (Lesson 9), and the name here is Caesar, "severed".

This is not a new idea. Tertullian, an early church writer, read the scene as the image of Caesar on the coin going to Caesar and the image of God on man going to God (De Idololatria). What this course adds is the rest of the vocabulary: the names, the Judges, and the court the passage is set in.

Matthew, Mark and Luke

The same scene is in Mark 12:13-17 and Luke 20:20-26. Mark adds a second question after the first: are we to give, or not to give? All three use the same three Greek words.

Check this yourself

  1. Read Matthew 22:15-22 in the BBE, with the chapter around it.
  2. Look up G1325 (dounai) and G591 (apodidomi) in Strong's. Note which word is in the question and which is in the answer.
  3. Look up G1504 (eikon) and see where the Greek Genesis uses it (Genesis 1:26-27).
  4. Ask whether the reading fits the words on the page. If it does not, say so.

What changed from Genesis 1

Lesson 5 says every story is the Genesis 1 picture, changed. The same image is here, but it sits on a different thing.

The last two rows show what the coin is doing. The original image is the reader's own judging, which sets the scene and its circumstances, and in Genesis 2:8 the man is put into Eden, "pleasure". On the coin the image is Caesar's, and the name means "severed". A demand arrives with it: "give tax." So the answer sorts the images. What carries the severed image goes back to Severed, and what carries the Judges' image is kept as theirs. The one being asked is named for salvation, and the story shows it: the yes-or-no trap has no way to catch him.

What changed is what carries the image. In Genesis 1 the image is on the one made, and rule comes with it. Here the image is on a coin, a thing that can be handed over, and the test is the same: whatever carries an image is marked as belonging to the one whose image it is. Caesar's image is on the coin. The image of the Judges is on you.

Remember: the question was whether to give, and the answer is whose image. What carries Caesar's image goes back to Caesar, and what carries the image of the Judges, the one made in Genesis 1:26-27, is the Judges'. The scene acts out the name Jesus, "Jehovah is salvation": the way out of a cut with two doors is the image. The coin was the test, and it is the same test for the one asking.

Common questions

What did Jesus mean by 'render unto Caesar'?

Jesus does not answer yes or no to the tax question. He asks whose image is on the coin. They say Caesar's, so it goes back to Caesar. In this course, what carries the image of the Judges (Genesis 1:26-27) is the Judges'.

Is 'render unto Caesar' about paying taxes?

The question put to Jesus was about tax, but his answer does not say 'pay' or 'do not pay'. He changes the question to whose image is on the coin. Some readers take the verse as an instruction to pay tax and others say it is not about tax at all.

Is 'render unto Caesar' about church and state?

The phrase is widely quoted that way, but the passage itself names no church and no state. It names a coin, an image, Caesar, and the Judges (theos, 'God'), and it applies one test: whose image is it?

What is the difference between 'give' and 'render' in Matthew 22?

The question in 22:17 uses the plain word for give (dounai, G1325). The answer in 22:21 uses apodidomi (G591), 'to give away, i.e. up, over, back'. The question is about giving, and the answer is about giving back.
