Bible Names and Places Are Codes

This is what makes the Bible different. In most books a name is just a label. In the Bible a name tells you what the state is like before the story starts, and a place name tells you what the ground is like. So read the meaning, not just the title.

No longer will your name be Abram, but Abraham, for I have made you the father of a number of nations. — Genesis 17:5 (BBE)

With the meanings put in:

No longer will your name be Exalted Father, but Father of a Multitude, for I have made you the father of a number of nations.

The verse explains the name itself. Abram is "exalted father" (Strong's H87) and Abraham is "father of a multitude" (H85). Growth is already in the name, and the story then plays it out.

The Bible tells you to read names this way

This is not a trick brought in from outside. The text sets up its own labels and then keeps using them the same way. Three places show it.

  • Genesis 1 names each thing as it is made. "Naming the light, Day, and the dark, Night" (Genesis 1:5, BBE). The arch is named Heaven (1:8), and the dry land is named Earth and the gathered waters Seas (1:10).
  • Babel gives the reason in the same verse.
    So it was named Babel, because there the Lord took away the sense of all languages and from there the Lord sent them away over all the face of the earth. — Genesis 11:9 (BBE)
    Babel (H894) comes from balal (H1101), "to mix, to confuse". The name is the event. Whenever Babylon comes up again, the story is back in the state the name describes, as in Jeremiah 29.
  • 1 Samuel 25 states the rule outright: "as his name is, so is he". See Nabal and Abigail below.

Isaac works the same way. Abraham laughs when he is told (17:17), the name is given (17:19), and Sarah says she has "cause for laughing" (21:6). Isaac is "laughter" (H3327, from tsachaq, H6711), and the story is full of it (the promise page).

The method, in four steps

  1. Find the name or place in Strong's.
  2. Read its meaning and put it in place of the title.
  3. Ask what state that meaning describes.
  4. Watch the story run in line with it, because Elohim works after its kind.

A person's name tells you the state being lived in. A place name tells you the ground it is lived on. Both count, so every page in this course reads both.

Worked example: put the meanings into a verse

Here is the same method on two verses from the working examples. First the BBE, then the BBE with the meanings put in.

Mary at the garden (John 20:1). Mary is Miriam (H4813), "rebellion", and Magdalene (G3094) is "a tower", something built up.

Now on the first day of the week, very early, while it was still dark, Mary Magdalene came to the place and saw that the stone had been taken away from it. — John 20:1 (BBE)
Now on the first day of the week, very early, while it was still dark, Rebellion, the Tower, came to the place and saw that the stone had been taken away from it.

Read that way, someone built up on rebellion stands at the start of the scene, in the dark of Genesis 1:2. The page then shows her staying in the garden after the others go home (Who was Mary Magdalene?).

The man from the field (Luke 23:26). Simon (G4613) is "hearing", from shama (H8085). Cyrene (G2956) is glossed "supremacy of the bridle" (Thayer's). Jesus (G2424) is "Existing One is salvation".

And while they were taking him away, they put their hands on Hearing of Supremacy-of-the-bridle, who was coming from the field, and made him take the cross after Existing-One-is-salvation. — Luke 23:26 (BBE, with the meanings put in)

The BBE says "country" where this puts "field". The Greek (agros, G68) is "a field (as a drive for cattle); genitive case, the country".

Now the verse is about hearing, brought in from the field, taking the tree after the identity. Shama is first used in Genesis 3, where the hearing hides and is held to account (Who carried the cross?).

Names working together in one scene

The clearest proof is a scene where several names meet and the plot follows all of them at once.

  • Noah's family (Genesis 9). "Rest" is uncovered. "Hot" tells it outside. "Name" and "Expansion" cover him without looking. The curse falls on "Humiliated", and Expansion is made great in the tents of Name. The plot is the meanings meeting each other.
  • Nabal's field (1 Samuel 25). "Fool" holds the fruitful ground. "Beloved" comes up with four hundred swords. "Source of Joy" steps between them. Nobody has to be told what will happen, because the names say it.
  • Esther. "Magnificent" is raised up and files a verdict against the Praised-line. "Star" holds her identity off the record until she puts it on, and then a second writing under the same seal answers the first.

How to keep it honest

A meaning is only worth using if you can check it. Every page in the course follows these rules.

  • The text first. If the verse explains the name (Abraham, Babel, Isaac), that explanation comes before any lexicon.
  • Then Strong's. Use the number and the gloss it gives. Where Blue Letter Bible or Thayer's gives a different gloss, say which one is being used. Machpelah is "a fold" in Strong's and "double" in Blue Letter Bible, and Cyrene's "supremacy of the bridle" is Thayer's, not Strong's own.
  • Do not force a link. Rufus is a Latin name (G4504), so no Hebrew link is drawn. Where the Greek or Hebrew behind a matching English phrase has not been checked, the page says it is a matching picture, not a word link.
  • Three questions for any scene. What is the ground, and what does its name mean? Who is standing on it, and what do their names mean? What has changed from the Genesis 1-3 picture?

Try it on a passage

  1. List every name and place in the passage.
  2. Look each one up in Strong's and note the number.
  3. Put the meanings into the verse in place of the titles, as in the two examples above.
  4. Read it again and ask what state each name describes.
  5. Watch what the story does, and check it against the verses.

Remember: the name is the code, and the story is the court making it real.
