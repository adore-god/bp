What Does "Ask and It Will Be Given" Mean? Matthew 7:7-11 With the Meanings Inserted

Short answer: the one asking is meant to rule the scene, not be ruled by it. In this course, asking is filing a claim about who you are, and the court rules to match what was filed. Matthew 7:9-10 tests this with two swaps, bread for a stone and a fish for a snake, and says neither happens. What you ask for is already under you, so it does not come back as something that takes charge of you. Read the passage first, then read it again with the meanings put in.

The passage (BBE):

Make a request, and it will be answered; what you are searching for you will get; give the sign, and the door will be open to you: Because to everyone who makes a request, it will be given; and he who is searching will get his desire, and to him who gives the sign, the door will be open. Or which of you, if his son makes a request for bread, will give him a stone? Or if he makes a request for a fish, will give him a snake? If you, then, being evil, are able to give good things to your children, how much more will your Father in heaven give good things to those who make requests to him? — Matthew 7:7-11 (BBE)

The same passage with the meanings put in:

Ask, and it will be answered; what you are seeking in order to find you will get; rap, and the entrance will be open to you: Because to everyone who asks, it will be given; and he who is seeking will get his desire, and to him who raps, the entrance will be open. Or which of you, if his son asks for bread, will give him a stone? Or if he asks for a fish, will give him a serpent? If you, then, being evil, are able to give good things to your children, how much more will your Father in heaven give good things to those who ask him?

The meanings used: Ask is aiteo (G154). Search is zeteo (G2212), and Thayer's lexicon opens its entry with "to seek in order to find". "Give the sign" is krouo (G2925), which Strong's gives as "to rap", meaning to knock. Door is thura (G2374), "a portal or entrance (the opening or the closure)". Bread is artos (G740), "bread (as raised) or a loaf", food of flour mixed with water and baked. Stone is lithos (G3037) and fish is ichthys (G2486). Snake is ophis (G3789), which Strong's gives as "serpent" and uses figuratively "as a type of sly cunning". Thayer says the Septuagint mostly uses it for the Hebrew nachash (H5175), the snake of Genesis 3:1. There are no names in these verses, so the meanings come from the verbs and nouns. See Lesson 9 for the method with names.

Two notes on the meanings. First, the BBE says "give the sign" for krouo in verses 7 and 8, where Strong's says "to rap, knock". The put-in version uses Strong's. Second, Leviathan, which the Job comparison below uses, is livyathan (H3882), "a wreathed animal", a serpent or large sea-beast. It is a different Hebrew word from the sea-beasts of Genesis 1:21, which is tannin (H8577). The two are linked by kind, not by spelling, and this page does not say they are the same word

What comes before: verses 1-6

Verses 7-11 sit in the middle of Jesus' speech, and the verses just before them set up the test.

Be not judges of others, and you will not be judged. For as you have been judging, so you will be judged, and with your measure will it be measured to you. — Matthew 7:1-2 (BBE)

The chapter opens on a measure: what you hand out is what comes back. Verses 3-5 add the bit of wood in your own eye, which has to come out before you can see clearly. Then verse 6:

Do not give that which is holy to the dogs, or put your jewels before pigs, for fear that they will be crushed under foot by the pigs whose attack will then be made against you. — Matthew 7:6 (BBE)

Something valuable is put before the wrong kind, and what comes back is an attack on the giver. Verses 7-11 then run the test the other way round: a request is made, and the question is whether the wrong kind comes back. The text does not join the two with a word, so this is how they sit together in this reading.

How to read it, step by step:

  1. Asking is filing a claim. This is the courtroom from Lesson 1. YHVH, your present awareness, puts forward an identity, the I AM, and Elohim, the judges, make it real (Lesson 2). The chapter states the rule itself in verse 2: "with your measure will it be measured to you" (Matthew 7:2, BBE).
  2. Ask, search, rap. Three moves, each with its result: ask and it is answered, search and you get, rap and the door opens. A door is an entrance (G2374), the way from one scene into the next, so in this reading the third move is the one that changes the scene. Verse 8 says "everyone". The result does not depend on who is asking, only on what is filed.
  3. Bread or stone. Both belong to day three. Bread is made from flour, and flour comes from the seed-bearing plants of Genesis 1:11. A stone is bare dry land (Genesis 1:9-10) with nothing planted in it. So the swap would put bare ground where the seed's result was asked for. The seed gives after its kind (Lesson 7), so that swap does not happen.
  4. Fish or snake. A fish is a water creature of day five (Genesis 1:20-21), the part of the scene that holds you and carries you through (Lesson 4). A snake is a beast of the field (Genesis 3:1), a land creature of day six (Genesis 1:24-25). They are different kinds, so one is not handed back for the other. The snake is also the one creature in Genesis that speaks to the woman, and she acts on what it says (Genesis 3:1-6). So a fish swapped for a snake would put a speaking creature in charge of the one who asked. That is a false filing, the pattern behind missing the mark.
  5. The one asking rules the scene. Genesis 1:26 puts "the fish of the sea" first in the list of what the man rules, and the land creatures, the snake among them, are on the same list. So both a fish and a snake are under the man (Lesson 6). Asking from that position is asking for something already under you, and verses 9-10 say it does not come back as something that rules you. Lesson 23 shows the reverse, the man listening to the snake.
  6. The measure comes back. Verse 11 ends on "good things". In Genesis 1 the court's verdict on what it made is "good" (Genesis 1:4, 1:10, 1:12, 1:21, 1:25). Verse 12 closes the loop: what you would have done to you is what you do, the same measure as verse 2, and it ends on "the law and the prophets" (Matthew 7:12, BBE). So ask in the kind you want, because the kind you hand out is the kind you get back.

What comes after: verses 12-27

Verse 12 closes the loop (step 6 above). The rest of the chapter keeps asking the same two things: which kind is this, and what is it measured by.

  1. The door again (verses 13-14).
    Go in by the narrow door; for wide is the door and open is the way which goes to destruction, and great numbers go in by it. For narrow is the door and hard the road to life, and only a small number make discovery of it. — Matthew 7:13-14 (BBE)
    The BBE says "door" here, but the Greek is a different word from verse 7. It is pyle (G4439), "a gate", and Thayer says it is used figuratively for "access or entrance into any state". So an entrance is shown twice, in two words: in verse 7 the door opens to the one who raps, and here there are two entrances and two roads, one wide and one narrow.
  2. Sheep's clothing (verse 15). "Be on the watch for false prophets, who come to you in sheep's clothing, but inside they are cruel wolves." One kind dressed as another. It looks like a sheep and is a wolf. See The creatures: states wearing skin.
  3. The fruit (verses 16-20).
    Even so, every good tree gives good fruit; but the bad tree gives evil fruit. It is not possible for a good tree to give bad fruit, and a bad tree will not give good fruit. — Matthew 7:17-18 (BBE)
    This is after its kind, said again in plain words. Genesis 1:11 has the fruit-trees giving fruit "after their sort", and here the tree is known by what it gives: "By their fruits you will get knowledge of them" (7:20). It is the same rule that keeps a fish from turning into a snake in verse 10, and verse 16 puts it as grapes not coming from thorns.
  4. Saying and doing (verses 21-23).
    Not everyone who says to me, Lord, Lord, will go into the kingdom of heaven; but he who does the pleasure of my Father in heaven. — Matthew 7:21 (BBE)
    Saying the name is not the same as holding the state. The ones in verse 22 list what they did "in your name", and the answer is "I never had knowledge of you: go from me, you workers of evil" (7:23, BBE). This is the measure of verse 2 again: the claim you actually hold is what comes back. Lesson 25 sets these same two verses beside Nabal.
  5. Two houses (verses 24-27). The one who hears the words and does them is "a wise man who made his house on a rock" (7:24, BBE). The one who hears and does not is "a foolish man who made his house on sand" (7:26, BBE). The same storm comes against both: the rain, the rush of waters and the winds (7:25, 7:27). One house "was not moved". The other "came down and great was its fall". Both heard the same words, so what decides it is what was done with them. Lesson 25 follows the wise and the foolish through this ending.
  6. The close (verses 28-29). "When Jesus had come to the end of these words, the people were surprised at his teaching, for he was teaching as one having authority, and not as their scribes" (7:28-29, BBE). Jesus' name means the Existing One is salvation, and the speech ends on how he spoke: with authority, not by quoting others.

What changed from Genesis 1

Lesson 5 says every story is the Genesis 1 picture, changed. Genesis 1 puts man over the creatures:

And the Judges said, Let us make man in our image, like us: and let him have rule over the fish of the sea and over the birds of the air and over the cattle and over all the earth and over every living thing which goes flat on the earth. — Genesis 1:26 (BBE)

The scene keeps all its parts: ground, seed, fish, snake. What matters is who is in charge. In Genesis 1 the man rules the creatures. In Matthew 7 the one asking stays over them, and the verses say what he asks for is not swapped for a voice that would rule him.

Compare Genesis 3 and Job. In each case the man is ruled by the scene and is not ruling it. In Genesis 3 the snake speaks and the woman follows its word. In Job the man speaks of those "ready to make Leviathan awake" (Job 3:8, BBE), a serpent or large sea-beast (H3882), and then says "Am I a sea, or a sea-beast, that you put a watch over me?" (Job 7:12, BBE). He speaks as if he is under the creature, when in Genesis 1 he was set over it. Matthew 7 is the other side of the same picture: the one asking is over the scene, and what he asks for does not come back as something that rules him.

Remember: the one asking is made to rule the scene, not to be ruled by it. A fish is not swapped for a snake, so what comes back does not take charge of the asker. In Genesis 3 and in Job the man is under the creature instead. What you hand out is the measure that comes back, and the verses before and after say the same thing.
